# Summarize personal goals without implying health

**For #95, multiple goals can contribute to a descriptive “progress toward your selected goals” number, not an overall-health score.** For adults aged 18+ pursuing general wellness, the reviewed evidence does not select scientifically optimal weights. Compare equal active-goal weighting with explicitly chosen user priorities before committing either; retain individual goal/domain results and explanations whichever you choose. Separate results without a total avoid cross-domain tradeoffs, but change your preferred numeric aggregate and require explicit approval. Goal Attainment Scaling (GAS) informs prospective goal definition; its clinical evidence does not validate a wellness formula. No new formula or weighting commitment follows from this brief.

## GAS supports goal definition, not wellness weights

Kiresuk and Sherman’s original GAS method defines individualized expected outcomes and ordered attainment levels, usually −2 through +2, with zero representing the expected outcome. Its goal weights express relative value. The authors’ claim that equal weighting loses little information without a decided preference is procedural guidance, **not an empirical comparison establishing optimal wellness weights**. The standardized composite also depends on distributional and inter-goal correlation assumptions; do not import its constants into openhealth ([Kiresuk and Sherman, 1968](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01530764)).

Clinical transfer needs restraint. Logan and colleagues reviewed **38 adult healthcare RCTs, 58% in rehabilitation and 24% in geriatric medicine**. Their findings concern heterogeneous implementation, not which consumer aggregation option performs best ([Logan et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9316030/)). Gaasterland and colleagues’ systematic review found insufficient validity evidence, particularly for drug-trial use; responsiveness alone does not establish validity or justify transfer across settings ([Gaasterland et al., 2016](https://bmcmedresmethodol.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12874-016-0205-4)). The supplied research verifies no direct comparison of equal versus user-priority weights for multiple goals in generally healthy adults. That is a bounded research gap, not a worldwide claim that relevant evidence cannot exist.

## Three options encode different tradeoffs

**Equal active-goal weighting** offers the simplest explanation: every included goal contributes equally. It avoids asking you to quantify importance, but still assumes unlike goals have comparable attainment scales and permits success in one goal to compensate for another’s deterioration. It is not equal domain weighting: choosing several activity goals gives activity more influence than choosing one sleep goal. Goal splitting and easier targets can therefore change the summary without equivalent improvement. This is a mathematical/design implication, not a verified estimate of consumer gaming.

**Explicit user-priority weighting** represents your stated values rather than hidden product priorities. It follows GAS’s relative-value rationale, but the reviewed papers do not establish superior validity or reliability. Explain the resulting contributions and record priority changes. Keep importance distinct from target difficulty: multiplying by difficulty can count it twice when difficulty already shapes the target. Neither option supplies scientific exchange rates between sleep, activity, nutrition, body, and wellbeing.

**Separate domain results plus a narrative summary, without a total**, avoid pretending these exchange rates exist. Keep numeric per-goal progress, showing attainment and deterioration together. A mental-health IOM study demonstrates a narrower alternative—one personally relevant goal alongside a selected standardized outcome measure—not evidence that a no-total wellness dashboard is superior ([Pesola et al., 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6878370/)). This option changes the preferred single longer-term number. If that preference remains firm, compare the two aggregate options for comprehension and faithful representation of priorities; do not describe either as validated wellness weighting.

## Stable goal definitions matter before arithmetic

Before combining goals, record each measure/unit, baseline value/date, direction, target, evaluation window, source, and scoring rule. **Ordinal attainment labels do not establish equal distances between levels**; averaging them introduces a measurement assumption. Tennant’s methodological critique supports caution, but only its abstract was verified in the notes ([Tennant, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17882728/)). Percentage-to-target calculations also need goal-specific justification, especially for maintenance or bounded targets; the reviewed evidence does not validate universal linear progress across five domains.

Choose and disclose whether overachievement stops contributing at attainment or continues contributing: the latter increases compensation, while the former hides additional progress unless shown separately. Preserve raw results and make deterioration visible. Keep missing, paused, not applicable, and zero progress distinct. Excluding missing goals changes the denominator; assigning zero penalizes missingness. State coverage and unavailable states without inventing an evidence-backed minimum-data threshold.

Version the goal set, baseline, targets, priorities, source changes, and aggregation rules. Start an annotated period when definitions change rather than splice incompatible values into an uninterrupted trend. Add domain coverage incrementally; unselected domains are not failures. Offer manual capture where meaningful and wearable inputs only with metric-specific justification; neither input route establishes cross-domain comparability. These are proposed safeguards, not a validated protocol.

## Source checks expose two note discrepancies

The supplied notes repeat Logan’s abstract claim that 66% omit score calculation; **Table 3 and the results instead report 11%, with 66% referring to baseline placement**. This brief excludes the disputed statistic. The supplied IOM link identifies **Pesola et al. (2015), not O’Cathain et al. (2019)**; its verified substantive findings remain usable under the corrected identity. Logan and IOM full texts were rechecked here; original GAS and Gaasterland findings rely on the notes’ full-text verification, and Tennant remains abstract-limited.

## Conclusion

The next decision is whose tradeoffs the number expresses—not which published constants to borrow. Resolve that choice and the goal-level measurement contract before #95 commits arithmetic; then test whether you understand why the summary changes and what it does not say about health.
