# Flag fewer changes, but make each one count

openhealth's rules should call a change "meaningful" only when four conditions all hold: a **7-day rolling mean** (not a single day) moves past the person's **own day-to-day noise band**, the move also clears a **practical floor**, it **persists** for several days, and the baseline it is compared against is **ready, same-device and weekday-neutral**. Each condition matters because the signals are noisy in very different ways. Resting HR from wearables varies by about **3 bpm a day** within a person. Sleep duration varies by more than **an hour**. Steps vary by **35–60%**. Weight swings about **0.35% every week**, peaking on Mondays. A naive "today vs your average" rule therefore fires every Monday, every winter and after every holiday. Simple arithmetic shows that ten rules with 2-SD daily triggers would produce a false alarm roughly **every four to five days per user**. The threshold numbers in this report come from athletes and large wearable cohorts. They are sensible engineering defaults, not validated wellness cutoffs, and no "smallest worthwhile change" for wellness outcomes exists. Copy should therefore promise "different from your usual", never "meaningful for your health". On delivery, the evidence supports the owner's decisions. Pushes reliably bring users back into the app for a short window. Their effect on behavior is weak and fades over weeks. "Smart" timing has not beaten simple fixed timing. Descriptive feedback on the user's own data is a recognized behavior-change technique. And both the platform rules and GDPR point toward opt-in consent and generic lock-screen text. The one decision the evidence pushes back on is the full-content weekly email, which needs an explicit risk disclosure at opt-in.

## Noise differs about a hundredfold across the five signals

The sports-science framework judges an individual's change against two yardsticks. The first is the **typical error**: the standard deviation of a person's repeated measurements ([Hopkins 2000](https://link.springer.com/article/10.2165/00007256-200030010-00001)). The second is a **smallest worthwhile change**. A measure can detect a worthwhile change only if its noise is smaller than that change. Buchheit's review of heart-rate monitoring gives athlete typical errors of about **10% for resting HR and 12% for Ln rMSSD** (HRV), with approximate smallest worthwhile changes of −2% and +3%. He also warns that "there is currently no evidence that changes greater than any fraction of the CV would actually be meaningful in practice" ([Buchheit 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2014.00073/full)). That caveat is why insight copy should describe a deviation from the person's usual range, not health significance.

Consumer wearable data in general adults behaves differently from athletes' standardized morning spot checks. In 92,457 Fitbit users across about 33 million person-days, the mean within-person SD of daily resting HR was about **3.0 bpm**, roughly 4.6% of a 65 bpm mean. The median weekly fluctuation was about 3 bpm, and about 80% of people never changed by more than 10 bpm in a week. Average resting HR differed between people by as much as **70 bpm** ([Quer et al. 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0227709)). That spread between people is the core reason population norms are useless here and personal baselines are mandatory. The other signals are noisier still. Pooled actigraphy data put within-person sleep-duration SD at **77 minutes** (CV about 19%) ([PMC9649840](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9649840/)), and 43% of US adults and children had a nightly SD of an hour or more ([Sci Rep 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10719297/)). Step counts showed a within-person CV of 0.355 in an aging cohort ([PMC13228750](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13228750/)), and CVs of 40–63% or more were common among 26,935 connected-device users ([PubMed 33856352](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33856352/)). Body weight is the quietest signal in relative terms, with a day-to-day SD of **0.53–0.69%** in healthy adults ([PMC10653631](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10653631/)). But that is about the same size as a week of real change.

Averaging over 7 days shrinks the noise by roughly √7, which gives the starting thresholds below. Autocorrelation in daily series makes the true standard error larger than these figures, so the thresholds sit deliberately above it.

| Signal | Within-person day-to-day noise | Approx. noise of a 7-day mean | Suggested starting trigger (unvalidated) | Comparison window |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Resting HR | ~3 bpm SD (wearables) | ~1.1 bpm | 7-day mean ≥ 3 bpm from baseline, plus persistence | 7-day vs trailing 4–8 weeks |
| HRV (Ln rMSSD) | ~12% CV (athletes only) | ~4–5% | Exceeds personal SD band and a ~3% floor; needs ≥ 3–4 readings/week | 7-day vs trailing 4–8 weeks |
| Sleep duration | ~60–80 min SD | ~25–30 min | 7-day mean ≥ 30–45 min shorter/longer | 7-day vs trailing 4–8 weeks |
| Steps | 35–60% CV | large | ≥ 20–30% drop in weekly total or 14-day mean | 14-day or weekly totals |
| Weight | 0.5–0.7% SD, 0.35% weekly cycle | ~0.2% | Smoothed slope over ≥ 3–4 weeks projecting > ~1–1.5% change | EWMA/slope over weeks |

The HRV row is the weakest. No large general-adult study of within-person variability in consumer nightly HRV turned up, so openhealth should estimate each user's HRV noise from their own data rather than borrow the athlete 12% figure.

## Persistence and practical floors do more work than clever statistics

Three families of methods map cleanly onto insight rules. The **Reliable Change Index** asks whether a two-point difference exceeds measurement error at about p<0.05 (|Δ|/S_diff > 1.96). Its framing, "larger than your typical day-to-day variation", is honest copy for a wellness app ([Jacobson & Truax 1991](https://sciences.ucf.edu/psychology/childrenslearningclinic/wp-content/uploads/sites/24/2013/08/Truax-Jacobson-pdf.pdf)). But it was built for pre/post psychometric scores and ignores regression to the mean ([Blampied 2022](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-cognitive-behaviour-therapist/article/reliable-change-and-the-reliable-change-index-still-useful-after-all-these-years/75AC9F09E738AC53924DA77AE4C66D7D)). Since wearables provide dozens of repeated readings per person, openhealth can compute the within-person SD directly and compare the means of two windows. It does not need to borrow a reliability coefficient. **Statistical process control** is the closer fit. It has been applied directly to individual patients' clinical variables, including blood pressure, asthma, renal function and diabetes ([Tennant et al. 2007](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK74277/); [Thor et al. 2007](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Application-of-statistical-process-control-in-Thor-Lundberg/8ddea25c22a0a84126b7650d81e952084b8f651c)). An individuals (XmR) chart, with limits at mean ± 2.66 × the mean moving range, suits a "sudden unusual day" rule ([analyse-it](https://analyse-it.com/docs/user-guide/process-control/shewhart-control-chart-rules)). EWMA or one-sided CUSUM suits "trending up or down". Both families assume independent observations, and their performance "deteriorates significantly under autocorrelation" ([J Quality Technology 2022](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00224065.2022.2139783)). Daily physiology is autocorrelated, so the nominal false-alarm rates of these charts will understate the real ones.

The most useful single template comes from Quer's Fitbit cohort. It flags resting HR more than **2 SD above the mean of the previous 20 readings, on at least 3 readings within 5 days** ([Quer et al. 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0227709)). The k-of-n persistence requirement is the cheapest and most effective false-alarm control available. Sending each threshold through an absolute practical floor (for example, 3 bpm) stops a person with an unusually tight baseline from getting alerts about trivial shifts. A cooldown or hysteresis after a rule fires makes one episode produce one insight instead of a daily repeat. Supplementary run rules, by contrast, are a trap. They catch smaller shifts but raise false alarms and shorten the in-control run length ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225775194_Statistical_Process_Control_using_Shewhart_Control_Charts_with_Supplementary_Runs_Rules)).

The false-alarm arithmetic shows why these controls are not optional. Assuming independence and normality, a one-sided 2-SD daily trigger fires on about **2.3% of normal days**, about once every 44 days per rule. Ten such rules running daily give each user a spurious insight about **every 4–5 days**. A two-sided 3-SD trigger fires on only 0.27% of days (an average run length of about 370), and adding persistence on 7-day means cuts the rate further. No study has measured false-alarm burden in consumer wellness insight engines. openhealth should therefore set an explicit budget itself. A reasonable target is that, across all rules combined, a user with stable data sees a spurious change insight no more than about once a month. Each rule's parameters should be simulated against historical or synthetic stable series before shipping to check it stays within budget. This budget follows from the arithmetic above and the platform cap of one push a day. No study has validated it.

## Calendars, devices and thin data cause most false alarms

Weekly and seasonal cycles are large enough to generate false insights on their own. Across about 73 million nights from 116,879 Withings under-mattress sensor users, weekend sleep ran **20–35 minutes longer** than weekday sleep, and northern-hemisphere winter sleep ran 15–25 minutes longer ([Scott et al. 2025](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/48/9/zsaf099/8112832)). Smart-scale users' weight peaked on Monday and bottomed out on Friday, a swing of **0.35% of body weight**. The Christmas period added 1.35%, and at least 0.35% of that was still there in March ([Turicchi et al. 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0232152)). Population resting HR follows an annual cycle of about 2 bpm, peaking in early January ([Quer et al. 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0227709)). A full 7-day rolling window cancels the weekday cycle by construction. Any rule that compares partial weeks or single days should compare like with like ("this Monday vs your usual Mondays"). A 60–90 day baseline partly absorbs seasonal drift. Year-over-year comparisons become possible only after a full year of data. Regression to the mean is a related trap: a "recovery" insight fired the day after an extreme reading is mostly describing noise, so improvement rules need the same persistence gate as decline rules.

Minimum data depends on what the rule estimates. A **mean** stabilizes quickly: 2–5 days for steps ([Sci Rep 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8100112/)), 2–7 nights for sleep, and at least 3 measurements a week for a valid weekly HRV mean ([Plews et al. 2014](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259319333_Monitoring_Training_With_Heart-Rate_Variability_How_Much_Compliance_Is_Needed_for_Valid_Assessment); [Buchheit 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2014.00073/full)). A person's **variability**, which sets the noise band itself, needs much longer: about **41–65 nights** for sleep ([PMC9649840](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9649840/)). Quer's cohort used the previous 20 readings and was restricted to people wearing the device at least 2 days a week, 20 hours a day. This points to two readiness gates. Rules comparing means need about 14 valid baseline days and 4–5 valid days in the current week. Rules that rely on a personal SD band need about 28–42 days. Until then, openhealth should show "building your baseline", which also fits the repo's explicit-readiness principle. Each signal needs a "valid day" definition, such as minimum wear hours for steps or a main sleep period present for sleep.

Device changes are the least-studied risk and probably the most common real-world one. Wearables compute resting HR with proprietary formulas; Fitbit, for example, estimates a "supine, just after waking" equivalent ([Quer et al. 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0227709)). A 2025 network meta-analysis found that step counts differ systematically between devices ([PubMed 42492741](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42492741/)). No evidence exists on calibrating devices within one person or on how long a re-baseline should run. The safe rule follows from SPC's Phase I/Phase II logic. Baselines are keyed by source and device. A device switch opens a new baseline, and trend rules are suppressed until that baseline clears its readiness gate. Series from different devices are never spliced together. A device switch can itself produce a data-quality insight ("new source detected; rebuilding your baseline"), which turns a false-alarm risk into one of the owner's planned insight kinds.

## Pushes buy minutes of attention, not lasting behavior change

Micro-randomized trials consistently find a real but short-lived effect of notifications. In the JOOL well-being app (n=1,255, 89 days), a push raised the chance of engagement within 24 hours by **3.9%**. The lift was **8.7% on weekends**, the best slot was 12:30 pm on weekends, and the effect did not decay over 12 weeks ([Bidargaddi et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6293241/)). In Drink Less (n=350), an 8 pm notification made opening the app in the next hour **3.5 times as likely**. Daily notifications did not speed disengagement compared with none, and new message text worked no better than repeated text ([Bell et al. 2023](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2023/1/e38342)). Behavior is a different story. In HeartSteps (n=37), walking suggestions raised 30-minute step counts by 107% early in the study, but that effect "diminished over time" to a 24% average ([Klasnja et al. 2019](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Efficacy-of-Contextually-Tailored-Suggestions-for-A-Klasnja-Smith/a825886eeb254a96cd3ae44e2699aedfc606b774)). Responses peaked about 10 minutes after the push and faded by about 40 minutes ([arXiv 2410.15049](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.15049v1)). The honest summary: an insight push reliably brings a user back to look at their data. Whether it changes anything beyond that is unproven. No trial has tested pushes carrying personal-data insights rather than generic suggestions, and none has run longer than about 13 weeks.

That fits the owner's cap of **at most one push per day**, and the evidence gives no reason to raise it. The only three-arm frequency trial found that "intelligent" sensor-timed and daily notifications got more views than occasional ones (about every 72 hours) but **no more app usage**. Discontinuation was 60% in the intelligent-timing arm, against 42% for daily and 39% for occasional ([Morrison et al. 2017](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0169162)). Users already receive about 63 notifications a day, and higher volume tracks with negative emotion ([Pielot et al. 2014](https://scispace.com/pdf/an-in-situ-study-of-mobile-phone-notifications-37xljon7zn.pdf)). App reviews describe notifications as a "double-edged sword": welcome when useful, timely and relevant, irritating otherwise ([Empirical Software Engineering](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10664-022-10222-6)). The practical consequences are a fixed, user-chosen delivery window rather than adaptive timing; "send nothing" as the default outcome of each daily decision (the JITAI framework treats it as a valid option, [Nahum-Shani et al. 2018](http://people.seas.harvard.edu/~samurphy/JITAI_MRT/jitai.html)); and per-rule mute and "not useful" rates as the main alert-fatigue metric. The owner's dismiss-and-mute control produces exactly that data. Feeding it back into each rule's false-alarm budget closes the loop between detection and delivery.

The weekly email digest has theoretical support but little direct evidence. The closest RCT used 6 weekly coaching emails rather than data digests, with only 47 participants. Only the email arm improved self-reported walking within its own group, but differences between groups were not significant ([Black & Brunet 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8729328/)). The digest's real value is structural: it batches lower-priority insights (streaks, milestones, minor changes) that would otherwise compete for the single daily push. No evidence exists on which day or time works best for a digest.

## Descriptive framing, opt-in defaults and private surfaces line up with the evidence

The owner's "what changed, versus what baseline, how certain" template is, in behavior-change terms, **feedback on behaviour (BCT 2.2) and feedback on outcomes (BCT 2.7)** ([Michie et al. 2013](https://www.sbm.org/UserFiles/file/Michie-etal2013BCTV193techniquesAddlFileTaxonomy.pdf)). A meta-analysis of 138 RCTs found that interventions increasing progress monitoring improved goal attainment by **d=0.40**, mediated by the monitoring itself. Effects were larger when progress was physically recorded ([Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/)). That moderator argues for every push and email linking to a persistent insight record in the app rather than standing alone. On framing, gain-framed messages beat loss-framed ones for prevention behaviors such as physical activity, though only slightly (**r=0.083**), with no effect on intentions ([Gallagher & Updegraff 2012](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Health-Message-Framing-Effects-on-Attitudes,-and-A-Gallagher-Updegraff/89fb73ce6ebdda6af939c6832bd96057058d7d07)). Since there is no behavioral upside to alarm framing, neutral wording such as "your 7-day resting HR is 4 bpm above your 6-week usual" is the right default. No trial has compared descriptive and prescriptive insight wording, and no study has measured whether wearable change alerts make healthy users anxious. Both are open questions.

Consent defaults carry ethical weight because defaults work: a meta-analysis put their effect at **d=0.68**. They are strongest when they signal what the designer endorses ([Jachimowicz et al. 2019](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/when-and-why-defaults-influence-decisions-meta-analysis-default-effects)). Turning health-change alerts on by default would tell users the app thinks they should be watching these signals. The platforms already require an explicit grant. Android 13+ requires the POST_NOTIFICATIONS runtime permission ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/notifications/notification-permission)). Apple's Guideline 4.5.4 says pushes must not be required for the app to work and "should not be used to send sensitive personal or confidential information" ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). GDPR treats health data as special category data, so where consent is the legal basis it must be explicit, specific and withdrawable ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/consent/when-is-consent-appropriate/)). That consent must cover sending derived insights through third-party processors such as APNs, FCM and the email provider. The owner's plan, with push off by default and offered in context after a useful in-app insight, matches all of this. No RCT has tested pre-permission prompts in context, but they are standard practice.

The generic lock-screen default is implementable on both platforms. Android's VISIBILITY_PRIVATE with a redacted setPublicVersion() shows text like "You have a new insight" while hiding the detail, though "the user always has ultimate control" over lock-screen visibility ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/notifications/build-notification)). On iOS, the alert text itself must be non-sensitive, and the **Passive** interruption level (iOS 15+) suits non-urgent insights because it delivers quietly to the notification list without lighting the screen ([WWDC21](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2021/10091/)). Insight pushes should never use Time Sensitive.

The full-content weekly email is where the owner's decision runs ahead of the privacy evidence. Email is unencrypted in transit and easily misdirected. US HHS guidance lets providers send health information by unencrypted email only after warning the individual of interception and misdelivery risk and honoring their stated preference ([HHS FAQ 570](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/570/does-hipaa-permit-health-care-providers-to-communicate-with-patients-by-e-mail/index.html)). openhealth is probably outside HIPAA, but that warn-and-honor pattern is the right model for the digest opt-in screen. It should state plainly that the email will contain health values and that email is not private. The digest must also stay purely a service message. Under the UK ICO's PECR guidance, adding promotional material to a service message turns it into direct marketing, with separate consent rules ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/direct-marketing-guidance/identify-direct-marketing/)). Apple applies the same split to pushes. "Try the AI coach" or "upgrade" lines therefore do not belong in a digest or an insight push. Whether the US FTC Health Breach Notification Rule applies to a self-hosted open-source deployment was not checked and needs separate legal review.

## Conclusion

The research changes the main question from "which statistical test?" to "what false-alarm rate will we tolerate, and who measures it?" The methods are all simple and well understood: 7-day means, personal SD bands, k-of-n persistence, practical floors, cooldowns and device-keyed baselines. What the literature lacks is any validated threshold or false-alarm budget for general adults on consumer wearables. openhealth therefore cannot import correctness. It has to build its own evidence. That means simulating each rule against stable historical series before launch, publishing each rule's parameters and expected false-alarm rate alongside its template (which suits an open-source project), and treating per-rule "not useful" and mute rates as the live calibration signal. The same discipline links detection to delivery. A daily push cap only protects users if the rules feeding it are rarely wrong. And since a push's effect lasts minutes, a user's lasting trust depends on how accurate each insight is, not on how often insights arrive.

Two owner decisions deserve a second look in light of the evidence. First, HRV rules should ship last and most conservatively: the only noise estimates come from athletes, and readiness requires several readings a week. Second, the full-content email digest is defensible only with a plain risk disclosure at opt-in and strictly non-promotional content, because email is the one channel where openhealth's careful lock-screen privacy defaults stop applying.
