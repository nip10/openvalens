# Ship transparent effort, not proprietary strain

**No strain, training-load, or weekly activity score has been validated against health outcomes in general adults when computed from wearable data, so openhealth should not ship a single "strain" number that claims to be one.** What the evidence does support is narrower. Foster's session-RPE (a 0–10 effort rating × minutes) converges moderately to strongly with heart-rate TRIMP methods, mostly in athletes and young recreational exercisers, and it is the only load method that works without a device. WHO-style weekly moderate-to-vigorous minutes rest on guideline-grade population evidence and can be computed from manual logs. HR-zone load (Edwards or Banister TRIMP) is a reasonable optional extra when heart-rate data exist. The branded scores are weaker than their marketing. WHOOP Strain is undisclosed and essentially unvalidated. Zepp's PAI is patented to 2034/2041, and its mortality data come from one developer group using back-calculated questionnaire answers. ACWR "sweet spots" and Foster monotony thresholds come from elite-sport cohorts and have been heavily criticised on methodological grounds. The honest product is three separate, labelled, formula-visible quantities: weekly active minutes against the WHO range, session effort points (sRPE), and optional heart-rate load. Change should be shown descriptively against the user's own recent weeks, with no injury-risk, overtraining, or life-expectancy claims. This sits alongside the readiness index, which is already decided as experimental. Load should be presented with less authority than readiness, not more.

## The load formulas are public and simple, but validated mostly in athletes

The classic internal-load models are easy to reimplement because they are just weighted minutes. **Banister TRIMP** multiplies duration by the heart-rate-reserve fraction and a sex-specific exponential weight: 0.64·e^(1.92·HRR) for men and 0.86·e^(1.67·HRR) for women. The weights were fitted to lactate curves ([Paulson et al. 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2015.00388/full); [Polar white paper](https://www.polar.com/img/static/whitepapers/pdf/polar-training-load-pro-white-paper.pdf)). **Edwards' summated HR zones** count minutes in five bands from 50–100% of HRmax and weight them 1–5 ([Borresen & Lambert 2008](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.3.1.16)). **Lucia TRIMP** needs lab-measured ventilatory thresholds. It was derived from seven professional Tour/Vuelta cyclists and is impractical for consumers ([Univ. Bath record](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/tour-de-france-versus-vuelta-a-espana-which-is-harder)). **Foster's session-RPE** is a CR-10 rating of the whole session × minutes. Its founding 2001 paper found it "highly consistent" with an HR-zone method across cycling and basketball, although sRPE scores ran systematically higher ([Foster et al. 2001](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/11708692)).

The validation pattern is consistent: convergent validity, mostly in athletes. A review of 36 studies reported sRPE correlations of **r = 0.52–0.99 with Banister TRIMP and 0.52–0.97 with Edwards**, with reliability ICCs mostly 0.69–0.95 ([Haddad et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5673663/)). Agreement depends on intensity. In elite skiers the correlation fell from **r = 0.70 at low intensity to 0.31 at high intensity** ([Yang et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11155691/)). In 33 ad-libitum trainers, the sRPE–TRIMP relationship over- or under-predicted depending on how much time people spent in low or high zones ([Borresen & Lambert 2008](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.3.1.16)). The closest study to openhealth's population is small: 25 recreationally active adults in their early twenties who were new to high-intensity functional training. sRPE tracked Edwards well (r = 0.889) and Banister only moderately (r = 0.561). Reliability was poor-to-moderate (ICC 0.586) and improved with familiarisation ([Crawford et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6162783/)). Correlation between two proxies is not accuracy, and neither proxy is a gold standard. **No study in these notes validates summed daily or weekly sRPE load against any wellness or health outcome in non-athlete adults.**

Two practical findings favour sRPE as openhealth's universal unit. First, it handles lifting where heart rate fails. Polar and Firstbeat both concede that HR-based load handles strength work poorly; Firstbeat writes that "EPOC may be low although the individual would be exhausted" ([Firstbeat EPOC white paper](https://www.firstbeat.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/10/white_paper_epoc.pdf)). Session RPE, by contrast, rose with %1RM even as total work fell (ICC 0.88) ([Day et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1519/R-13113.1); [Sweet et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1519/14153.1)). Second, timing is forgiving. Ratings taken anywhere from 5 minutes to 24 hours after cycling did not differ ([Christen et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2015-0438)), though some functional and resistance sessions show early-rating drift ([PMC6162408](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6162408/)). The 30-minute rule is convention, so a same-day manual entry is legitimate data.

## Vendor strain scores disclose little and have validated less

| Score                        | What is disclosed                                                                                                    | Independent validation found                                      | Usable by openhealth?                                             |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| WHOOP Strain                 | 0–21 non-linear "Borg-inspired" scale; HR zones plus accelerometer "muscular load"; bands Light 0–9 to All out 18–21 | One single-athlete 2026 case study (n = 1)                        | No. Formula is proprietary; a lookalike invites false equivalence |
| Firstbeat EPOC / Garmin load | EPOC model structure; r² = 0.79 vs measured EPOC in 32 adults on a cycle ergometer (company-affiliated)              | None for Garmin's acute-load product in general adults            | No. Proprietary; lab fit is not free-living accuracy              |
| Polar Training Load Pro      | Openly Banister TRIMP (cardio) + RPE × duration (perceived) + 7/28-day averages                                      | None product-specific; "based on scientifically accepted methods" | The pattern, yes. It uses public methods anyone can implement     |
| Zepp PAI                     | Patent discloses HRR → exponential weighting → VO₂-calibrated 0–100 scale                                            | Outcome studies all by developer group, using questionnaire data  | No. Active patents and likely trademark                           |

WHOOP publishes only the scale's shape: "It takes more stress to move from a score of 16 to 17 than 4 to 5" ([WHOOP developer docs](https://developer.whoop.com/docs/whoop-101/)). An independent documentation review of 14 composite scores from 10 manufacturers found that "none of the companies disclose specific details about the algorithms or formulae used." It also found the composites "have undergone little to no validation in the peer-reviewed scientific literature" ([Doherty et al. 2025](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/teb-2025-0001/html)). That review explicitly excluded dedicated training-load metrics, so it says nothing either way about Garmin or Polar load. The only peer-reviewed WHOOP Strain study found is a single elite junior swimmer whose Strain tracked sRPE load ([JFMK 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/jfmk11030369)). Firstbeat's EPOC evidence is one company-affiliated cycle-ergometer study with a pooled error of about 14 ml/kg ([Firstbeat white paper](https://www.firstbeat.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/10/white_paper_epoc.pdf)). Polar matters because it shows a commercial vendor shipping **standard published methods with names attached** (Banister for cardio load, Foster for perceived load) rather than an invented index ([Polar white paper](https://www.polar.com/img/static/whitepapers/pdf/polar-training-load-pro-white-paper.pdf)). Openhealth can do the same and go further by showing the arithmetic.

## PAI's longevity evidence is real but questionnaire-based and patent-locked

PAI is the most outcome-linked score in this group, and its evidence is narrower than its branding suggests. Every outcome paper is an observational cohort from the Wisløff/Nauman/Nes group: HUNT, Cooper Clinic ACLS, and China Kadoorie Biobank. In every one, **PAI was back-calculated from self-reported leisure-activity questionnaires**, not measured with heart-rate devices. In China Kadoorie (Hammer et al.), intensity categories were mapped to fixed values of about 44/73/83% of heart-rate reserve ([Hammer et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9655296/)). The associations are genuine but modest, and they shrink as cohorts grow. All-cause mortality for PAI ≥100 vs 0 was **HR 0.79 in ACLS men, non-significant 0.85 in ACLS women** ([Nauman et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32560967/)), and **0.93 in 443,792 Kadoorie adults** ([Nauman et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34865822/)). The 100 threshold is a calibration choice: the patent scales it to a target VO₂peak of about 45 ml/kg/min for men and 35 for women ([EP2921105](https://patents.google.com/patent/EP2921105A1/en)). The group's own 2025 coronary-disease paper found **PAI 51–99 associated with slightly lower risk than ≥100** (aHR 0.80 vs 0.86) ([Nauman et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39499588/)). The widely repeated "eight extra years" figure appears in NTNU press material ([NTNU CERG](https://www.ntnu.edu/cerg/personal-activity-intelligence)). The primary abstracts report 2.7–6.6 life-years for specific observational contrasts.

Interventional evidence is thin. The only long-term RCT, ONWARDS, gave all three arms a PAI tracker and had no tracker-free control. Over 18 months, accelerometer MVPA **fell from 69.7 to 60.2 min/day** in all arms ([Sagelv et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40546750/)). The inventor consults for PAI Health Inc ([Hammer et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9655296/)). The patent family has been reassigned to Beijing Shunyuan Kaihua Technology and remains active, with US11806120B2 running to 2041 ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/EP2921105A1/en)). Openhealth should neither name nor clone PAI. A clone would carry legal risk, and an HR-stream "PAI" was never the exposure measured in the cohorts anyway.

The public-domain alternative has much stronger evidence. WHO 2020 recommends **150–300 minutes of moderate or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity per week**, and states that "some physical activity is better than none" ([Bull et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33239350/)). A pooled analysis of 661,137 adults found that meeting the minimum captured "nearly the maximum longevity benefit" ([Arem et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25844730/)). Step counts have meta-analytic dose-response data, with risk plateauing around **6,000–8,000 steps/day at age ≥60 and 8,000–10,000 below 60** ([Paluch et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35247352/)). Weekly MVPA minutes can be computed directly from a manual log, from Health Connect exercise sessions, or from HR-zone minutes. That makes them the natural weekly activity number for an app with wearable-optional parity.

## Load ratios and monotony do not survive outside elite sport

The acute:chronic workload ratio came from elite cricket, rugby league, and AFL cohorts ([Gabbett 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26758673/)). The 2016 IOC consensus endorsed it alongside a <10% weekly-increase rule and an ACWR "ideally" between 0.8 and 1.3 ([Soligard et al. 2016](https://stillmed.olympics.com/media/Documents/Athletes/Medical-Scientific/Consensus-Statements/2016_How-much-is-too-much-load-sport-risk-illness-part-1.pdf)). Methodologists then took it apart. Including the acute week in the chronic window produces a **spurious correlation of about r = 0.52 even with no true relationship** ([Lolli et al. 2019](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/320847733_Mathematical_coupling_causes_spurious_correlation_within_the_conventional_acute-to-chronic_workload_ratio_calculations)). Simulated seasons with no built-in effect still yielded "significant" injury associations. Optimal windows varied across 336 tested combinations, and four acute-load calculations gave 1,340–3,450 AU on the same data. The critics advise practitioners to stop using ACWR quantitatively for injury prevention ([Impellizzeri et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7534938/); [Impellizzeri et al. IJSPP 2020](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/341936245_AcuteChronic_Workload_Ratio_Conceptual_Issues_and_Fundamental_Pitfalls)). The field remains split. Some reviewers still claim strong evidence in elite athletes ([Zouhal et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8138569/)), and team-sport reviews report associations without a causal or universal safe zone ([Griffin et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-019-01218-2)). No formal post-2016 consensus has withdrawn ACWR, and no ACWR study in general multi-activity adults appeared in these searches. The nearest population is 874 novice runners. Those who increased weekly distance by more than 30% had **HR 1.59 (95% CI 0.96–2.66)** for distance-related injuries only, and no difference for injuries overall ([Nielsen et al. 2014](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2014.5164)). That supports "build up gradually." It does not support a numeric threshold.

Foster's monotony (mean daily load ÷ SD) and strain (weekly load × monotony) come from one observational study of 25 experienced athletes. Its outcome was self-reported "banal illness," and its thresholds were individual and identified after the fact ([Foster 1998](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Monitoring-training-in-athletes-with-reference-to-Foster/59629df5a87418e5653956d54bf8630a102544ae)). The arithmetic is also unsuited to everyday adults. A person with steady daily activity has a small SD, so monotony climbs toward infinity, and it is undefined when SD is zero. A metric that labels a consistent walker "highly monotonous" punishes the behaviour the guidelines encourage. "Overtraining" is off-limits for the same reason. The ECSS/ACSM consensus treats overtraining syndrome as a diagnosis of exclusion and says no marker "meets all criteria to make its use generally accepted" ([Meeusen et al. 2013](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Prevention,-diagnosis-and-treatment-of-the-Joint-of-Meeusen-Duclos/66660afacc013f8c12bf1a86b46f7ea191a69e31)).

## Wrist sensors make heart-rate load an estimate, and some users break it

Any HR-based load inherits sensor error, and the error grows exactly where load matters. Consumer wrist devices averaged **7.2 bpm MAE at rest and 10.2 bpm during activity** against ECG ([Bent et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7010823/)). Concordance ranged from 0.99 for a chest strap down to 0.52 for one wrist watch ([Gillinov et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Gillinov+variable+accuracy+wearable+heart+rate+monitors+aerobic+exercise)). A meta-analysis found wrist PPG under-reading by **7.26 bpm during resistance training** and 4.55 bpm during cycling ([J Sports Sci 2020](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2020.1767348)). One small study found error on darker skin reaching 16.5 bpm at high intensity, though an earlier study at lower intensity found no skin-tone effect ([Hung et al. 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0318724); [Bent et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7010823/)). Some newer fitness watches perform much better in controlled lab testing ([PMC9445511](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9445511/)). HR zones are only about 12–20 bpm wide, so errors of this size move minutes between zones. **No wearable brand measures energy expenditure accurately** ([Fuller et al. 2020](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2020/9/e18694/)), with errors of roughly 30–50% ([Shcherbina et al. 2017](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4426/7/2/3)). Calorie-based load should therefore never drive a score. Accelerometer-only trackers misclassify a large share of light and moderate activity and miss cycling and upper-body work ([PMC13542415](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13542415/)).

Some users invalidate HR load entirely. Beta-blockers blunt heart rate, while the relationship between RPE and oxygen uptake stays stable ([PubMed 8776008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8776008/)). As a result, HR-based scores undercount effort and RPE remains usable. Atrial fibrillation and pacemakers create the same problem. Banister's coefficients are binary by sex, so a person who declines to give sex needs a documented fallback, and Edwards' zones need only HRmax. Missing days also corrupt every rolling metric, because an unsynced day looks identical to a rest day.

## What openhealth can ship, and how to word it

The defensible design keeps three quantities separate rather than fusing them into one opaque "strain."

| Quantity                                    | Formula (shown in-app)                                      | Inputs                                                                               | Default status                                                                    | What it may be compared to            |
| ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| **Active minutes this week**                | moderate min + 2 × vigorous min                             | Manual log with talk-test/RPE intensity, Health Connect sessions, or HR-zone minutes | Primary weekly activity number                                                    | WHO 150–300 range; user's own goal    |
| **Session effort points**                   | effort rating (0–10) × minutes, summed per day/week         | One question after any session, manual or device-logged                              | Primary load number; works for lifting, swimming, yoga                            | User's own previous 4 weeks only      |
| **Heart-rate load** (optional)              | Edwards zones (default) or Banister TRIMP, labelled by name | HR stream, HRmax (user-editable), HRrest                                             | Shown only with HR data and no user-declared HR-altering medication or arrhythmia | User's own history on the same device |
| Steps/day and strength volume (descriptive) | raw counts; sets × reps × weight                            | Phone/watch; manual lift log                                                         | Context, never converted into load points                                         | Raw history                           |

Change over time should be shown as plain comparison, not a ratio with zones. For example: "This week's effort points: 1,840, compared with an average of 1,210 over your previous four weeks." Compute the baseline from weeks that exclude the current one, so it avoids mathematical coupling. Require a minimum count of logged days and weeks before any comparison appears, and show a "still calibrating" state for the first few weeks. Crawford's data suggest effort ratings stabilise with familiarisation, but no study fixes the exact number of weeks, so treat any value as a product choice. When a jump is large, use neutral copy such as "A bigger jump than usual. Many people find building up gradually easier to sustain." Never use "injury risk," a red zone, or a fixed percentage threshold presented as science. Monotony can appear, if at all, only as a descriptive phrase ("your days were very similar this week"). It needs a minimum-SD guard and should carry no illness meaning.

On regulation, wording does most of the work. FDA's January 2026 general-wellness guidance keeps products in wellness when they avoid naming conditions, clinical thresholds, and diagnostic framing. It also allows a neutral "evaluation by a healthcare professional may be helpful" when values fall outside wellness ranges ([Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/fda-issues-revised-guidance-on-general-wellness-products)). Under EU MDR, qualification turns on intended purpose as stated in labelling and marketing. Software for "life-style and well-being purposes is not a medical device," but software meant to predict or prevent injury can be ([OpenRegulatory on MDCG 2019-11](https://openregulatory.com/mdcg/mdcg-2019-11)). For an open-source project, the README, docs, and UI copy define that intended purpose. Applying this guidance to load scores specifically is an inference, and a regulatory professional should confirm it.

| Avoid                                                 | Use instead                                                                                                                                                                             |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "Strain: 17.4" / "PAI 100"                            | "Effort points: 340 (effort 7 × 48 min)"                                                                                                                                                |
| "Injury risk: high" / ACWR 1.6 in red                 | "Well above your recent 4-week average"                                                                                                                                                 |
| "Overtraining detected"                               | "Your recent load is higher than usual; consider how you're recovering"                                                                                                                 |
| "Adds 8 years to your life" / "scientifically proven" | "In large population studies, adults in this range tend to have lower rates of heart disease and early death. These studies show association, not proof that activity alone caused it." |
| "Heart-rate load: 0" for a no-device user             | "No heart-rate data. Effort points and active minutes still count."                                                                                                                     |

The safeguards follow from the evidence. Every number shows its formula, inputs, source device or "manual," algorithm version, and data completeness. Manual and wearable paths get full web and mobile parity on active minutes and effort points, and HR load is clearly an add-on rather than a "better" score. HRmax and HRrest are user-editable. Users can declare HR-altering medication or an arrhythmia without being asked to explain why; the declaration suppresses HR load and gives no clinical interpretation. Missing days stay missing rather than becoming zeros. No metric is converted into another's units without disclosure: steps never become effort points, and TRIMP is never added to sRPE. Load feeds the experimental readiness index only as a disclosed input, never as a validated predictor. There are no cross-user leaderboards or percentiles, because the sensor and population evidence supports within-person trends only.

## Conclusion

Strain's evidence problem is less about weak data than about outcome validation for a population that hasn't been studied. Every validated piece found here sits at a different level from what consumer strain scores imply. Session-RPE is validated as a measure of perceived session load, mostly in athletes. Guideline minutes are validated as a population exposure linked to mortality. HR methods are validated as approximations of each other. None of them is validated as a personal daily verdict. This turns openness into the product advantage. A score whose formula a user can read, recompute, and question is the only kind that can honestly claim what the evidence supports, and openhealth can offer that where WHOOP and Zepp cannot.

The genuine open questions are empirical and could become openhealth's own contribution: whether sRPE is reliable in sedentary and older adults, how manual-log completeness distorts weekly comparisons, and whether any week-over-week change signal predicts anything in non-athletes. Instrument these as deterministic, versioned calculations with provenance from day one. The app can then evaluate its own metrics later, with consent, instead of borrowing authority from elite-sport cohorts and patented indices.
