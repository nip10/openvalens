# Ship guideline facts, not longevity promises

OpenValens can honestly offer a small, well-defined set of preventive-care items: the age- and sex-based core of the USPSTF A/B list for US users, England's NHS call/recall screening programmes and NHS Health Check for UK users, and Portugal's SNS cancer-screening programmes for EU/Portugal users, along with per-jurisdiction vaccine packs. Each item should be stored as a pinned, dated, human-reviewed fact with a deep link, not as copied guideline text. Almost everything the longevity ecosystem promotes falls outside that core. That includes vitamin D, fish oil and multivitamins "for longevity", annual full blood panels, whole-body MRI and multi-cancer blood tests. Large trials and high-certainty reviews show none of these improve hard outcomes in healthy adults. Only a few extra tests have guideline backing: Lp(a) once in a lifetime, and CAC when a statin decision is uncertain. Reminders work: Cochrane-grade evidence shows patient reminder/recall raises vaccination by about 8 percentage points. Decision aids also work for "individual decision" items like PSA. That makes a three-state design (due / discuss / informational) the right one. The binding constraints are regulatory and licensing, not evidence. In the US, age/sex/behaviour-filtered recommendations from established authorities sit under FDA enforcement discretion. Under the EU MDR, anything that computes personal risk or reacts to lab values is very likely class IIa medical device software. USPSTF and NICE licences forbid verbatim reuse in profit-making or AI contexts. GDPR treats smoking, pregnancy and family history as special-category data. One factor cuts across all of this: the US source of truth is itself unstable. The USPSTF was "largely defunct" for over a year before being restaffed in September 2026, mostly with specialists, so the US pack must be pinned to dated statements rather than "whatever USPSTF says today."

## Thirty US items shrink to about a dozen that age and sex alone can drive

The current USPSTF A/B list contains about 30 adult-relevant services ([USPSTF A and B Recommendations](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation-topics/uspstf-a-and-b-recommendations)). Many depend on risk factors an app cannot safely infer. Only a subset can be triggered by age and sex alone: **colorectal screening at 45–75**, **biennial mammography at 40–74 (B, 2024)**, **cervical screening at 21–65 (A, still the 2018 statement)**, blood pressure for all adults, HCV at 18–79, HIV at 15–65, depression, anxiety (≤64), unhealthy alcohol and drug use, osteoporosis for women 65+, and falls-prevention exercise at 65+. Other items need one additional, sensitive input. Lung screening needs **20 pack-years at 50–80**. AAA screening needs "ever smoked" for men 65–75. Prediabetes screening needs overweight or obesity at 35–70. Statins need CVD risk at 40–75 ([USPSTF A&B](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation-topics/uspstf-a-and-b-recommendations)). Cervical screening is the clearest example of why intervals matter. The 2018 statement allows cytology every 3 years from 21–29. From 30–65 it allows cytology every 3 years, or hrHPV alone or cotesting every 5 years. It also carries D grades (do not screen) for women under 21, women over 65 with adequate prior screening, and after hysterectomy with the cervix removed. The page says the topic "is being updated" ([USPSTF Cervical](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/cervical-cancer-screening)). PSA at 55–69 is a C (individual decision) and PSA at 70+ is a D ([AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/afp-community-blog/entry/task-force-to-revisit-prostate-cancer-screening-recommendations.html)). Hearing screening at 50+ ([USPSTF](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/announcements/final-recommendation-statement-screening-hearing-loss-older-adults)) and vision screening at 65+ ([USPSTF](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/impaired-visual-acuity-screening-older-adults)) are I statements, meaning the evidence is insufficient. None of these three should ever become a "due" reminder.

The US list looks stable on paper, but the body behind it is not. After _Kennedy v. Braidwood_ confirmed the HHS Secretary's authority over the Task Force, meetings were cancelled from July 2025. Vice-chairs were dismissed in May 2026. AHRQ support staff fell from about **300 to 84**. On 17 September 2026 eight new members were appointed, five of them specialists, led by a pediatric hematologist-oncologist, and no meeting date was set ([STAT](https://www.statnews.com/2026/09/17/kennedy-names-eight-new-members-uspstf-task-force/); [Fierce Healthcare](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/regulatory/kennedy-appoints-8-new-us-preventive-services-task-force-members)). The adult vaccine schedule is similarly contested. Search-level reporting describes COVID-19 moving to shared clinical decision-making, pneumococcal and shingles vaccination at 50+, and a March 2026 federal court stay on changes made since Kennedy took office ([AAFP 2026 schedules](https://www.aafp.org/about/blog/2026-immunization-schedules); [CDC adult schedule](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines/hcp/imz-schedules/downloads/adult/adult-combined-schedule.pdf)). These details were not confirmed against primary documents and must be checked before encoding. The honest response is to pin each US rule to a named statement and release date, and to require human review before adopting anything the reconstituted panel publishes. Where CDC and professional-society vaccine schedules diverge, the app should cite both.

The UK and Portugal run organised programmes that invite citizens themselves, and their parameters differ materially from the US:

Three design consequences follow. First, UK and Portuguese reminders should read "expect and attend your invitation," not "book a test." The health system owns recall, and OpenValens is a supplement to it. Second, the EU Council Recommendation is non-binding, so it is a fallback only. National programmes like Portugal's are what users are actually offered. Third, England's rules are not automatically the rules for Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland, so UK packs should be per-nation. Several Portuguese and EU parameters (the colorectal interval, the exact age ranges) come from search snippets rather than fetched primary pages, and need verification before launch.

Vaccines need their own per-jurisdiction rule pack with date-based seasonal rules. In the UK, Shingrix is being phased in from 65 and 70, pneumococcal is a single dose at 65, and RSV is offered at 75–79 ([NHS England SW toolkit](https://www.england.nhs.uk/south/wp-content/uploads/sites/6/2025/08/Adult-Vaccination-Programmes-Toolkit-SW-2025-26-v5.0.pdf)). Portugal gives Td boosters at 10, 25, 45 and 65, then every 10 years ([DGS – PNV](https://www.dgs.pt/em-destaque/novo-programa-nacional-de-vacinacao2.aspx)). Dental, vision and hearing reminders should not use invented fixed intervals. NICE CG19 sets adult dental recall by risk, from **3 to 24 months**, so "every 6 months" is not guideline-based ([NICE CG19](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/n/nicecg19guid/pdf/)). The right default is the interval the user's dentist assigned, bounded to that range.

## Grade by source type and treat rules as versioned, reviewed data

USPSTF letter grades map cleanly onto user-facing bands. A and B are described as services with "high or moderate net benefit" that the Task Force "most highly recommends implementing" ([USPSTF A&B](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation-topics/uspstf-a-and-b-recommendations)). NHS, SNS and EU programmes are policy decisions about organised programmes, not graded evidence statements. Forcing a letter-grade equivalent onto them would be dishonest. The defensible scheme is:

| Source signal                            | App band                      | Behaviour                                        |
| ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| USPSTF A/B; CDC/JCVI/PNV routine vaccine | Recommended                   | Due/overdue reminders                            |
| NHS/SNS organised programme              | Offered by your health system | "Expect your invitation" reminder                |
| USPSTF C (e.g., PSA 55–69)               | Discuss with clinician        | No countdown; decision-aid link; record decision |
| USPSTF I (vision, hearing)               | Evidence uncertain            | Informational only                               |
| USPSTF D                                 | Not recommended               | Suppress or label                                |
| NICE/consensus intervals (dental)        | Practice guidance             | User-configured interval                         |

GRADE certainty ratings should not be merged into these bands. GRADE rates certainty of evidence and strength of recommendation, which differ from USPSTF's net-benefit grades. GRADE's plain-language conventions are still the right vocabulary for describing certainty in text. Its tested wording uses "will", "probably" and "may" for high, moderate and low certainty, and users prefer "the evidence is very uncertain" over "we are uncertain" ([GRADE guidelines 26](<https://www.jclinepi.com/article/S0895-4356(19)30416-0/fulltext>)).

Maintenance matters as much as the initial content. USPSTF topics are revised on cycles of several years, and the dates on the current list run from 2018 to 2025. National programmes change by policy decision, as when Portugal extended cervical screening to 69 in October 2024 ([DGS Norma 09/2024](https://www.dgs.pt/normas-orientacoes-e-informacoes/normas-e-circulares-normativas/norma-092024-de-17102024-programa-de-rastreio-de-base-populacional-do-cancro-do-colo-do-utero1.aspx)), and when England added lung screening at 55–74 to its national list ([NHS screening](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/nhs-screening/)). Machine-readable feeds are thin. USPSTF offers a "Prevention TaskForce" REST/JSON API queryable by age, sex and behavioural risk factors ([USPSTF API](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/apps/api.jsp)). Its availability after the AHRQ cuts is unverified, and it covers only the US. No equivalent feed was found for the NHS, DGS or the EU. The robust pattern is a local, versioned rule pack, with the API used at most as a diff check. Each rule records:

- jurisdiction, issuing body and statement URL;
- grade or programme status;
- release date and retrieval date;
- population criteria;
- `content_version`, `reviewed_by`, `reviewed_at` and `next_review_due`.

A quarterly scan of USPSTF, NHS screening, DGS normas and CDC schedules, plus an annual seasonal-vaccine update, fits observed change rates. Rules past their maximum review age (for example 12 months) should be flagged stale, and their reminders suppressed rather than shown as outdated advice. This fits the repo's existing provenance and auditability rules.

Licensing pushes toward the same "facts plus link" design. The USPSTF copyright notice allows redistribution only "without any changes to the work". It bars reproduction "for a fee" or incorporation "into a profit-making venture". It requires citation of the USPSTF page and an AHRQ non-endorsement disclaimer for adaptations ([USPSTF Copyright Notice](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation-topics/copyright-notice)). A search snippet calling USPSTF publications "public domain" conflicts with this notice and should not be relied on. NICE's UK Open Content Licence is UK-only. It forbids altering recommendation wording or structure, excludes display next to advertising, and does not cover AI use, which needs separate permission ([NICE UK Open Content Licence](https://www.nice.org.uk/reusing-our-content/nice-uk-open-content-licence)). Two features are therefore unsafe: an AI feature that summarises NICE text, and a hosted commercial OpenValens tier that ships verbatim USPSTF statements. Short paraphrased eligibility facts (ages, intervals, grades) with a deep link carry much lower risk. Whether such facts are free of copyright still needs legal confirmation. DGS/SNS and EU content licensing was not researched.

## Longevity stacks and full panels fail the trials that tested them

The large primary-prevention trials are consistently null for popular supplements. VITAL randomised about 25,000 US adults to 2,000 IU vitamin D3 daily. Over a median 5.3 years it found no reduction in invasive cancer (**HR 0.96, 0.88–1.06**) or major CVD events (**HR 0.97, 0.85–1.12**) ([VITAL findings](https://www.vitalstudy.org/findings.html); [Manson et al.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960076019305424)). D-Health gave 21,315 Australians aged 60–84 monthly high-dose D3 and found no reduction in all-cause mortality. Cancer-mortality point estimates were numerically higher in the treatment group ([Neale et al., Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol](<https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landia/article/PIIS2213-8587(21)00345-4/abstract>)). The Endocrine Society's 2024 guideline suggests **against routine 25(OH)D testing** in all populations it studied, and declined to define adequacy or prevention targets. Healthy adults under 75 are advised to take the recommended daily allowance ([Endocrine Society](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/vitamin-d-for-prevention-of-disease)). An app that prompts vitamin D tests or shows an "optimal level" contradicts that guideline.

Fish oil shows the same pattern, plus a harm signal. VITAL's omega-3 arm missed its primary CVD endpoint (**HR 0.92, 0.80–1.06**) ([VITAL findings](https://www.vitalstudy.org/findings.html)). ASCEND found 8.9% vs 9.2% serious vascular events in people with diabetes ([ACC](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Clinical-Trials/2018/08/25/02/19/ASCEND-Omega-3)). STRENGTH stopped for futility at **HR 0.99** with more atrial fibrillation ([JAMA](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2778119)). REDUCE-IT's positive result came from prescription EPA in high-risk patients. Its mineral-oil comparator raised LDL-C by a median 10.9%, so part of the "benefit" may be harm in the placebo arm. The result does not transfer to over-the-counter fish oil ([Circulation](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.122.059410)). A meta-analysis of 81,210 participants associated omega-3 with **about 25% higher AF risk**, rising at doses above 1 g/day ([Circulation](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.121.057464)). For multivitamins, the USPSTF gives an **I** grade, and it gives a **D** to beta-carotene and vitamin E, where beta-carotene's harms outweigh its benefits ([USPSTF](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/vitamin-supplementation-to-prevent-cvd-and-cancer-preventive-medication)). COSMOS found no multivitamin effect on invasive cancer or major CV events in 21,442 older adults ([COSMOS](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9170475/)). Its COSMOS-Mind substudy found only a modest cognitive-score benefit ([Alzheimer's & Dementia](https://alz-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/alz.12767)).

Two supplements earn a low-certainty "optional" label, but not a longevity claim. Creatine improved memory, attention and processing speed in meta-analysis, with effects varying by age and health status, and no effect on global cognition ([Xu et al.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11275561/)). Memory gains were clearer in older adults ([Prokopidis et al.](https://academic.oup.com/nutritionreviews/article/81/4/416/6671817)). The case for creatine is strongest alongside resistance training. Magnesium shortened sleep-onset latency by about 17 minutes in three small, bias-prone trials (low to very low certainty) ([Mah & Pitre](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12906-021-03297-z)). A 2026 review still calls the evidence mixed ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42661485/)). The tolerable upper intake for supplemental magnesium is **350 mg/day**, and people with renal impairment face higher risk ([NIH ODS](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/)). Logging supplements the user chooses is defensible. Recommending them as longevity interventions is not.

The "annual full panel" fails on high-certainty evidence. Cochrane's review of 15 trials and 251,891 participants found general health checks leave total mortality unchanged (**RR 1.00, 0.97–1.03, high certainty**), along with cancer mortality (RR 1.01) and CVD mortality (RR 1.05, moderate certainty). The review names unnecessary tests and treatments as the expected harm ([Cochrane](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD009009.pub3/full)). Denmark's Inter99, which combined screening with five years of tailored counselling, found no effect on heart disease, stroke or mortality at 10 years ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25329220/)). Choosing Wisely Canada advises against annual screening blood tests unless the patient's risk profile indicates them ([Choosing Wisely Canada](https://choosingwiselycanada.org/toolkit/ordering-routine-blood-work/)). The app should store and display panels users already have, with reference ranges and "discuss abnormal values with a clinician" framing. It should not nudge users to order them. No guideline position on fasting insulin, ferritin or HOMA-IR in asymptomatic adults was found, so the app should take no stance on those markers rather than invent one.

Advanced imaging and multi-cancer tests are where longevity marketing diverges most from evidence. Across 5,373 asymptomatic people, whole-body MRI produced **critical incidental findings in 13.4%** and indeterminate findings in another 13.9%, many of them false positives. The review authors concluded it should not be offered outside research ([Kwee et al., JMRI](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jmri.26736)). The NHS-Galleri RCT (about 142,000 adults aged 50–77) **missed its primary endpoint** of fewer stage III–IV cancers. Stage IV diagnoses fell in secondary analyses, stage III unexpectedly rose, and no mortality data exist yet ([NHS-Galleri](https://www.nhs-galleri.org/trial-updates/nhs-galleri-trial-reports-fewer-diagnoses-of-the-most-advanced-cancers)). An FDA advisory panel in September 2026 voted 6–4 on effectiveness and 7–2 that benefits outweigh risks. Critics noted the test misses about 85% of stage I cancers. Galleri remains a laboratory-developed test that FDA has not approved ([MDDI](https://www.mddionline.com/ivd/fda-advisory-panel-narrowly-backs-multi-cancer-blood-test-despite-effectiveness-concerns)). Two extra tests do have guideline backing. The European Atherosclerosis Society recommends measuring **Lp(a) at least once in every adult's lifetime**, because the level is largely genetic ([EAS, Atherosclerosis 2023](<https://www.atherosclerosis-journal.com/article/S0021-9150(23)00182-X/fulltext>)). ACC/AHA 2019 calls CAC scoring "reasonable" for intermediate-risk, and selected borderline-risk, adults when a statin decision is uncertain ([Circulation](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/cir.0000000000000678)). CAC is conditional and clinician-mediated, not a general longevity scan. No guideline mandating routine ApoB testing in all adults was found.

Exercise is the one area where influencer advice and guidelines agree. WHO recommends **150–300 minutes of moderate (or 75–150 minutes of vigorous) activity weekly plus muscle strengthening on 2 or more days**, with multicomponent balance training at 65+ ([WHO 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7719906/)). Cardiorespiratory fitness is a strong observational predictor of mortality ([Kodama, JAMA 2009](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/1108396)). No trial shows that training toward a VO2max percentile beats meeting activity guidelines, and wearable VO2max estimates add measurement error. VO2max therefore belongs on screen as an informational fitness indicator, not as a target. No peer-reviewed accuracy audit of Huberman, Attia or Johnson exists. The one academic study of Huberman content measured engagement, not accuracy ([JMIR Form Res](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10731552)). Critiques converge on one pattern: the core lifestyle advice matches guidelines, while supplement stacks, extensive biomarker testing, whole-body MRI and off-label drugs exceed the evidence, often with commercial conflicts ([Slate](https://slate.com/technology/2024/03/andrew-huberman-huberman-lab-health-advice-podcast-debunk.html)). The app should never attribute an item to a personality. Where influencer advice overlaps a guideline, the app should cite the guideline. Most trial populations were 50–60+, so applying these findings to 18–40-year-olds is indirect, and the copy should say so.

The resulting content tiers are:

| Tier                             | Items                                                                                                                     | Behaviour                                       |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Guideline-backed                 | WHO activity and strength; Lp(a) once; jurisdictional screening and vaccines                                              | Eligible for reminders                          |
| Conditional / clinician decision | CAC at intermediate risk; ApoB displayed as a risk marker                                                                 | Informational with clinician framing            |
| Low-certainty optional           | Creatine with training; magnesium for sleep onset; vitamin D at RDA level                                                 | "May" wording, effect sizes, harms; opt-in only |
| Not recommended without symptoms | Whole-body MRI; routine full panels; routine 25(OH)D; fish oil or multivitamin for longevity; beta-carotene and vitamin E | Label explicitly; never remind                  |
| Investigational                  | Galleri/MCED; off-label longevity drugs                                                                                   | Informational only; no nudges                   |

Tiers C–E should never have reminders switched on by default. Any supplement plan item should first pass hard interaction checks for anticoagulants, kidney disease and pregnancy. These checks are an inference from the evidence: no study specifically tested "experimental" labelling in consumer apps, and the anticoagulant interaction claim still needs a primary source.

## Reminders lift uptake about 8 points; decision aids belong on "discuss" items

Patient reminders have strong support. A Cochrane review of 75 studies found reminder and recall vaccinated **about 8% more people** than no reminder. All modalities worked and telephone worked best ([Cochrane](https://www.cochrane.org/about-us/news/reminding-people-about-vaccinations-can-increase-rates-immunization)). For cervical screening, more personalised invitations, letters and telephone reminders increased uptake ([Staley et al., Cochrane](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD002834.pub3/full)). Text messages help less reliably. Across nine screening studies they raised absolute rates by **0.6 to 15 points**, with possibly small effects for colorectal screening ([Uy et al., JMIR](https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S1438887117002849)). In one RCT, targeted SMS raised 30-day FIT completion from 53.7% to 64.2%, though the gap narrowed to about 5 points by 126 days ([M-TICS](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11325104/)). In another, **replacing postal reminders with SMS lowered participation from 21.9% to 17.2%** ([JMIR mHealth 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11736219/)).

Two cautions follow. Nearly all this evidence comes from health-system recall, where the sender knows who is due. A self-hosted tracker used by already-engaged people will probably see smaller effects; that is plausible but untested, and no RCT of reminders delivered by a consumer health-record app was found. The substitution result also means OpenValens must present itself as an addition to NHS and SNS invitations, never a replacement. The design features with evidence behind them are:

- **personalisation**: the named test, due date and how to book;
- **a follow-up prompt** after the first reminder;
- **multiple channels**: the app already has Telegram and email;
- **low-friction actions**: done, snooze, not applicable.

For C-grade items like PSA, the evidence favours decision support over nudging. Stacey et al.'s Cochrane review of 105 RCTs (31,043 participants) found decision aids improve knowledge and the accuracy of risk perception. They also reduce decisional conflict and the number of people who stay undecided, with no adverse effects ([Cochrane](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD001431.pub5/full)). The UI should therefore keep three states visibly distinct:

1. **Routine items** get due and overdue reminders.
2. **Individual-decision items** get no countdown. Instead they show "talk with your clinician," link to an established decision aid such as the Ottawa inventory ([OHRI](https://decisionaid.ohri.ca/cochsystem.html)), and record "decided: yes / no / later." "Decided no" is a legitimate end state that stops reminders.
3. **Not-recommended and insufficient-evidence items** are informational only.

Linking to existing decision aids rather than building a personalised benefit/harm calculator also matters for regulation. A calculator crosses into medical device territory, as the next section explains.

## Regulators allow guideline display but treat personal risk computation as a device

In the US, FDA's _Device Software Functions and Mobile Medical Applications_ guidance (September 2022) lists functions that "may meet the definition of medical device" but fall under **enforcement discretion**. Appendix B item 6 covers software that uses "age, sex, and behavioral risk factors to provide patient-specific screening, counseling, and preventive recommendations from well-known and established authorities". Item 11 covers user-configured medication reminders. Reminders for scheduled appointments count as non-device administrative functions ([FDA guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/80958/download)). This is discretion, not exemption. The Cures Act non-device CDS carve-out applies only to software aimed at health professionals. FDA's 6 January 2026 CDS revision "continues to focus exclusively on HCP-facing CDS". It offers nothing for patient decision support, chatbots or AI ([Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/5-key-takeaways-from-fdas-revised-clinical-decision-support-cds-software-guidance)). The revised General Wellness guidance, issued the same day, allows a prompt that "evaluation by a healthcare professional may be helpful". It bars references to specific diseases, clinical thresholds, and prompting "specific clinical action … including through alerts" ([Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/fda-issues-revised-guidance-on-general-wellness-products)). So "get screened for colorectal cancer" relies on Appendix B, not on the wellness policy. Lab-value triggers ("your LDL is X; discuss statins") go beyond Appendix B's examples. These 2026 details come from law-firm summaries, because the FDA PDFs could not be fetched.

The EU is stricter. The MDR device definition explicitly includes "**prevention**" and "**prediction, prognosis**". MDCG 2019-11 rev.1 (June 2025) lists "an app that provides a **10-year risk of cardiovascular disease from data input by a lay user**" as medical device software. It adds that software intended to prevent illness by analysing physiological parameters can be **class IIa** under Rule 11a. It excludes "generic diagnostic or treatment pathways (not directed to individual patients)" and "wellness or fitness apps". It also states that risk of harm "is not a criterion" for qualification ([MDCG 2019-11 rev.1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en)). That gives three options with different risk levels:

- **Option A: user-entered due dates with reminders.** This is close to non-device in both the US and the EU.
- **Option B: age- and sex-filtered display of cited public guidance.** This is covered by FDA discretion. In the EU it is ambiguous, because filtering by the user's own age is arguably "directed to individual patients". An intended purpose worded as information and education ("shows what public bodies publish for people of your age and sex; it does not assess your risk") narrows the risk without removing it.
- **Option C: computed risk scores, lab-triggered or family-history-tailored recommendations.** This is very likely class IIa medical device software in the EU, and falls outside US wellness and CDS carve-outs. It should be avoided, or placed behind a regulated pathway.

The intended purpose is judged from all claims, including README text, app-store copy and in-app wording. "Not medical advice" disclaimers do not override functions that show a medical purpose ([MDCG 2019-11 rev.1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en)). In the UK, the MHRA's stand-alone software guidance dates from July 2023 and includes appendices on calculators. An AI-specific framework is expected in 2026 ([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/medical-devices-software-applications-apps); [Burges Salmon](https://www.burges-salmon.com/articles/102icot/mhra-publishes-guidance-for-software-and-ai-as-a-medical-device/)). No Portugal-specific (INFARMED) software guidance was found. The MDR applies directly.

Open-source status helps in the EU only at the margins. The MDR's "making available on the market" means supply "in the course of a commercial activity, whether in return for payment or free of charge" ([TÜV SÜD MDR text](https://de-mdr-ivdr.tuvsud.com/Chapter-I-Scope-and-definitions.html)). Johner Institute reads this as excluding code that is not distributed commercially ([Johner](https://blog.johner-institute.com/regulatory-affairs/placing-on-the-market/)). A hosted SaaS, a paid tier or paid support probably makes someone a manufacturer. US device status turns on intended use, not commerce. No FDA, MDCG or MHRA text addresses open-source or self-hosted software directly, so legal advice is required. The EU Product Liability Directive 2024/2853 brings software into scope from **9 December 2026**. It excludes free and open-source software supplied outside commercial activity ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/2853/oj)), which leaves the same commercial-arm question.

Clinical safety governance is not legally required for a self-hosted consumer app, but NHS DCB0129 is the best template for it. NHS England is reviewing both DCB0129 and DCB0160 ([NHS England Digital](https://digital.nhs.uk/data-and-information/information-standards/governance/latest-activity/standards-and-collections/review-of-digital-clinical-safety-standards-dcb0129-and-dcb0160)). The template gives:

- **a named clinical reviewer** who signs off each content release;
- **a hazard log** covering wrong population match, stale guidelines, wrong date of birth or sex, false reassurance ("nothing due"), pregnancy contexts, channel failure and jurisdiction mismatch;
- **a user-facing changelog** when a rule affecting the user changes.

Data protection limits which risk-factor inputs are worth collecting. Under GDPR, smoking history, pregnancy and family history are data concerning health (Art 4(15)) and therefore special category (Art 9). Processing them needs explicit consent for specified purposes, purpose limitation, minimisation, data protection by design, and a DPIA at scale ([EUR-Lex GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)). Family history also contains health data about relatives who never consented, so it should be kept coarse ("first-degree relative with colorectal cancer under 60: yes/no") and never shared. A person self-hosting for themselves probably falls under the household exemption (Art 2(2)(c)). Anyone hosting for family, clients or organisations becomes a controller. In the US, the amended FTC Health Breach Notification Rule (effective 29 July 2024) covers non-HIPAA apps whose records can "draw information from multiple sources". It counts unauthorised disclosure as a breach ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule)). A hosted OpenValens instance importing from Health Connect, labs and calendars likely qualifies. Minimisation does double duty. Every clinical input dropped also strengthens the "generic information" case under MDCG step 4 and FDA Appendix B. Each risk-factor field should therefore be optional, collected only when a specific displayed rule needs it, tagged with its purpose, excluded from AI features without separate consent, and (for pregnancy) set to expire. Washington's My Health My Data Act, UK DPA 2018 Schedule 1 and EDPB health-app guidance were not researched and need review.

## Conclusion

The evidence and the regulation point the same way. The items with the strongest evidence are age/sex-triggered screening, vaccines, activity guidelines and Lp(a) once. Those are also the items that are cheapest to ship legally, provided they appear as cited facts from established authorities and are not calculated by the app. Every step toward the longevity-app model (personal risk scores, lab-triggered nudges, optimal-range overlays, supplement recommendations) does two things at once. It moves away from trial evidence, and it moves toward EU class IIa device status and US enforcement exposure. OpenValens's honest advantage is restraint made visible: provenance, review dates and "evidence uncertain" labels shown in the UI. That is something commercial longevity products have structural reasons not to offer.

The less obvious risk is source instability, not regulatory overreach. With the USPSTF restaffed by a largely specialist panel and the US vaccine schedule under litigation, the US is no longer the stable anchor it was. A rule engine that auto-syncs to "current USPSTF" could quietly import politically driven changes. Pinned statements, human sign-off and "stale content means suppressed reminders" are therefore core safety features, not bureaucracy. Before launch, the open items are: legal review of EU intended-purpose wording and the commercial-arm question; primary-source verification of the Portuguese, EU and US vaccine parameters that came from search snippets; and a decision on whether any hosted offering will exist. That last decision determines whether OpenValens becomes a GDPR controller, an FTC-covered health-record vendor and an MDR manufacturer.
