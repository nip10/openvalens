# Benchmark against public tables, never your neighbours

openhealth can show some honest "how you compare with people your age and sex" benchmarks, but far fewer than the wearable market suggests, and the usable ones are mostly manual measurements, not wearable metrics. Real, redistributable adult age×sex percentile tables exist mainly for **body measures (waist, weight, height, BMI) and resting pulse from NCHS/NHANES, which are public domain**. Grip strength comes from NHANES-derived and CC-licensed UK data. Lab-measured VO2max comes from the FRIEND registry, which is copyrighted and comes from a convenience sample. For the metrics wearables actually produce (nocturnal HRV, sleep-or-wake resting HR, estimated VO2max, wrist step counts), there is either no published percentile table or the reference was measured so differently that placing a wearable value on it misleads the user. The psychology adds a second problem. Percentile feedback motivates some people, demotivates those far below the reference, and can pull above-average users back toward the mean. The metrics with the cleanest data, weight and BMI, are also the ones most tied to weight stigma and disordered eating. An opt-in community aggregate inside a self-hosted instance cannot be made both private and useful, because household and small-org cohorts are too small to resist differencing, and differential-privacy noise at that scale wipes out the signal. The defensible design is self-trend first, published reference bands second, rank as an opt-in extra, no ranking of body size, and no intra-instance community benchmark.

## Four metric families have tables you can legally ship

The strongest sources are US federal reports. NCHS Vital and Health Statistics Series 3 No. 46 publishes **N, mean, SE and selected percentiles of weight, height, waist and other circumferences by sex, age and race/Hispanic origin for NHANES 2015–2018** ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/series/sr_03/sr03-046-508.pdf)). A newer edition covers August 2021–August 2023 ([CDC Series 3 No. 50](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/series/sr_03/sr03-050.pdf)). NCHS National Health Statistics Report No. 41 gives **resting pulse reference data for all ages from a normative sample of 35,302 NHANES participants examined 1999–2008** ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhsr/nhsr041.pdf)). Its exact percentiles, adult age bands and measurement protocol were not extracted in this research, so they need checking before implementation. Both reports can be embedded in an open-source repo: NHANES material "is in the public domain and may be reproduced or copied without permission; citation as to source, however, is appreciated" ([NHANES citation page](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/NhanesCitation.aspx)). The NCHS data user agreement limits use to statistical analysis and forbids re-identification attempts ([NCHS](https://cdc.gov/nchs/policy/data-user-agreement.html)).

Grip strength is the best-covered functional metric. Wang, Bohannon et al. 2018 published US normative values with percentiles for ages 18–85 from NHANES 2011–2014. Means range from **49.7 kg (dominant hand, men 25–29) to 18.7 kg (non-dominant hand, women 75–79)** ([JOSPT](https://www.jospt.org/doi/abs/10.2519/jospt.2018.7851)). Dodds et al. 2014 pooled **60,803 observations from twelve British studies** into life-course centile curves ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25474696/)). It is in PLoS One, which is open access, but its CC BY license was not confirmed on the article itself. For VO2max, the FRIEND registry's 2022 update is the only credible adult source found. It gives **percentiles by decade from 20 to 79, separately for treadmill and cycle, from directly measured gas-exchange tests** ([Mayo Clin Proc](<https://www.mayoclinicproceedings.org/article/S0025-6196(21)00645-5/fulltext>)). It is a lab registry rather than a population sample, and it is journal-copyrighted. Linking out or asking permission is safer than copying the tables.

Everything else is weaker. For steps, NHANES 2005–2006 hip-ActiGraph data give a US adult mean of **9,676 steps/day uncensored but 6,540 after censoring low-intensity counts** ([Tudor-Locke 2009](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/26282350_Accelerometer-Determined_Steps_per_Day_inUS_Adults)). Smoothed p5–p95 curves exist only for adults 60+ ([PubMed 23913932](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23913932/)). The newer NHANES wrist data have no agreed step algorithm yet ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.11040v1)). The large wearable cohorts publish means and curves, not reusable tables. Quer et al. 2020 analysed **92,457 Fitbit users (mean RHR 65.5 ± 7.7 bpm)** and found RHR higher in women at every age, rising until about 50 ([PLoS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0227709)). Natarajan et al. 2020 studied **8.2 million Fitbit users** and found that HRV declines with age, but that RMSSD shows no comparable sex difference ([Lancet Digital Health](<https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landig/article/PIIS2589-7500(20)30246-6/fulltext>)). Fitbit sleep studies report that women and younger users sleep longer ([PMC6399225](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6399225/); [JMIR 2025](https://www.jmir.org/2025/1/e71718)). The underlying vendor data cannot be redistributed. For HRV, the best clinical synthesis is a **pooled mean RMSSD of 50 ms with a 19–75 ms between-study range**, and the authors note that values depend on RR-editing method ([Nunan 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-8159.2010.02841.x)). That is not an age×sex percentile table. Consumer "HRV by age" percentile charts such as [Welltory](https://welltory.com/blog/what-is-a-good-hrv-range) cite no traceable primary dataset and should not be used.

No open-source project was found that publishes a curated, licensed set of adult wellness percentile tables. There are NHANES access libraries ([nhanesdata](https://github.com/kyleGrealis/nhanesdata)) and a youth-only accelerometer percentile repo that shows how to derive percentiles from microdata with code ([nhanes-youth-mims-percentiles](https://github.com/skwon10/nhanes-youth-mims-percentiles)). That leaves an open niche. openhealth could ship a versioned reference-distributions dataset computed from NHANES public microdata with reproducible, survey-weighted code. Each table would record source cycle, method, population and license. This is how to cover blood pressure, labs and self-reported sleep without depending on anyone's copyrighted table.

## Measurement mismatch breaks most wearable comparisons

A percentile only means something when the user's value was measured the way the reference was. On that test, manual entries mostly pass and wearable metrics mostly fail. Waist, weight, height and grip strength are comparable to NHANES if the user follows the same protocol, so the app should show NHANES-style measurement instructions next to the input. Grip protocol matters too: NHANES and NIH Toolbox values differ ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331539286_Handgrip_Strength_A_Comparison_of_Values_Obtained_From_the_NHANES_and_NIH_Toolbox_Studies)).

Wearable VO2max is the clearest failure. A meta-analysis of 14 validation studies found that resting-state wearable algorithms **overestimate VO2max by 2.17 ml/kg/min with 95% limits of agreement of −13.07 to +17.41**. The authors concluded that "the estimation error at the individual level is large" ([Molina-Garcia 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9213394/)). Apple Watch validations report **mean absolute errors near 7 ml/kg/min and MAPE around 13%** ([PMC13141568](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13141568/); [PLoS One 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0323741)). Garmin underestimates highly trained athletes by about 6.3 ml/kg/min ([PMC12881131](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12881131/)). An error that size is roughly the width of a FRIEND percentile band within one age decade, so a watch estimate placed on FRIEND could land one or more quintiles from the truth. This is an inference from the cited error sizes, not a published threshold. The most that can be defended is a coarse "below / near / above typical" band with an explicit uncertainty note.

HRV and resting heart rate fail for a subtler reason: the measurement context differs. Wearable RMSSD is usually overnight PPG during sleep. Clinical norms come from 5-minute supine ECG and vary with editing method ([Nunan 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-8159.2010.02841.x)), and HRV also shifts with time of day and activity ([MobiHealthNews on Natarajan 2020](https://www.mobihealthnews.com/news/fitbit-study-finds-heart-rate-variability-fluctuates-age-gender-time-day-and-activity-level)). Fitbit's daily RHR is a proprietary algorithm aimed at the supine on-waking value ([Quer 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0227709)). The NHANES figure is a clinic pulse. Placing a sleeping wearable RHR on the NHANES curve would probably flatter users systematically. That direction is inferred, and no study in the notes quantified the offset. Steps have the same problem: the reference used hip-worn devices, and a single censoring choice moves the mean by about 3,100 steps/day ([Tudor-Locke 2009](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/26282350_Accelerometer-Determined_Steps_per_Day_inUS_Adults)). Wrist counts compared against that are noise dressed as a rank. Population and era matter as well. NHANES is US 1999–2023, FRIEND is US lab-referred, Dodds is British, and Fitbit cohorts are self-selected device owners. Non-US users should be told which population they are being compared with.

## Rank feedback motivates some users and harms others

The behavioural evidence supports caution more than enthusiasm. Personalised normative feedback works in addiction research by contrasting a person's behaviour with actual peer norms ([PMC8016245](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8016245/)). The evidence for physical-activity apps is mixed and depends on the person. In daily studies of inactive college students using Fitbit, participants mostly chose upward comparison targets, and **motivation fell on days they picked targets furthest above themselves** ([Arigo et al. 2023](https://humanfactors.jmir.org/2023/1/e41239/)). That is exactly the setup a "top 10% for your age" or "vs 20-year-olds" display creates for someone well below the reference. Rank-only feedback also risks the boomerang effect: people already above the norm move back toward it when shown only descriptive data. In the classic energy study, an injunctive cue (a smiley face) prevented this ([Schultz et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01917.x)), and alcohol research suggests injunctive messages partly remove boomerang effects ([Frontiers 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02180/full)). No trial was found that tests percentile-for-age displays of fitness or biomarkers in adults. Every prediction about openhealth's users is extrapolated from students and other domains.

Comprehension is a separate risk. Even among highly educated people, 16% misjudged which of 1%, 5% and 10% is largest, and low-numeracy readers rated "10%" as lower risk than "10 out of 100" ([PMC2596505](https://europepmc.org/api/getPdf?pmcid=PMC2596505)). Less numerate people are more swayed by framing ([Peters et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01720.x)), and expert guidance favours absolute values and natural frequencies over relative formats ([Gigerenzer et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2008.00033.x)). "Top 10%" invites confusion between percentile and percentage, and between rank and health risk. "+10% vs 20-year-olds" is a relative format anchored to a reference group the user can't see.

Age framing has both upside and distortion. In a workplace RCT of 2,844 employees, a heart-age tool produced better activity, smoking, blood pressure and waist outcomes than percentage risk or usual care ([via JMIR Cardio 2021 review](https://doi.org/10.2196/31056)). Experimentally, though, low-risk people put themselves in a higher-risk category **47% of the time with heart age versus 23% with absolute risk** ([Bonner et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0272989x15597224)). Fitness age rests on a validated mortality-predicting model from HUNT ([NTNU CERG](https://www.ntnu.edu/cerg/vo2max)), but no trial of showing it to users was found.

Body metrics are where harms concentrate. A 2025 systematic review of 27 studies (N=10,584) found that fitness and diet tracker use was linked to **more disordered eating in 7 of 8 studies and more excessive exercise in all 7 that measured it**, with large effects among MyFitnessPal users (d=0.91–1.06). The same review found that **every experimental study showed no causal effect**, so reverse causation (at-risk people choosing trackers) is plausible ([Moody et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12547374/)). Tracking for weight or shape reasons was linked to more restraint and purging than tracking for health. In one eating-disorder sample, **83% of MyFitnessPal users said the app at least somewhat contributed** to their disorder (same source). Weight stigma harms health independently of BMI ([Tomiyama et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6092785/)), and internalised weight bias is associated with _less_ self-monitoring ([PMC7646152](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7646152)). This gives an irony: the metric with the best public-domain percentile data, BMI and waist, is the one openhealth should not rank. A "top X% for BMI" display amounts to a score for body size and offers little to act on. The safer form is a value against a clinical range, in neutral language, with a global setting to hide body metrics and comparisons.

The display these findings support has four parts. First, the user's value and its trend against their own baseline. Second, a shaded guideline range where one exists. Third, an optional "middle 50% of adults your age and sex" band with the reference population and year named. Fourth, plain category text paired with a goal or health framing rather than bare rank. A percentile rank would be secondary, opt-in, and never offered for weight, BMI or body fat.

## Community aggregates leak inside small self-hosted cohorts

An opt-in "community" benchmark computed within one openhealth instance is not privacy-safe, and no threshold fixes it. Aggregates are not automatically anonymous. Dinur and Nissim showed that releasing enough accurate statistics allows the underlying records to be reconstructed ([Kamath notes](https://www.gautamkamath.com/CS860notes/lec2.pdf)). The US Census Bureau **exactly reconstructed microdata for 46% of the population from published 2010 tables and re-identified over 50 million people by linkage**, which led it to adopt differential privacy ([arXiv 2111.04671](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.04671); [Census Bureau](https://www2.census.gov/about/policies/2020-03-05-differential-privacy.pdf)). Aggregate genomic frequencies alone exposed individual cohort membership ([Homer et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1000167)). Regulators require anonymisation to prevent singling out, linkability and inference, and treat k-anonymity and aggregation as carrying residual risk ([WP29 Opinion 05/2014](https://ec.europa.eu/justice/article-29/documentation/opinion-recommendation/files/2014/wp216_en.pdf)).

In a household or small organisation these attacks are trivial. The ICO notes that **the NHS standard for strong anonymisation is k=5**, and that people can be singled out within a family from year of birth alone ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/data-sharing/anonymisation/how-do-we-ensure-anonymisation-is-effective/)). A household's age×sex cells are usually below five, and members already know each other's ages and sexes. Percentiles are order statistics, so at small n a reported quantile often _is_ someone's value. Any recomputation after one person joins reveals that person's contribution by differencing, whatever the minimum-cell rule. The ICO's "motivated intruder" test and its warning that data "may be personal data in your hands" fit this case exactly: the spouse, housemate or employer-admin is the motivated intruder with the richest possible auxiliary knowledge ([ICO 2025 summary](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/global-data-hub/2025/spotlight-on-the-uk-data-landscape/gdh---new-ico-guidance-on-anonymisation-and-pseudonymisation)). In a 100-person workplace instance, the power imbalance adds its own concern.

Differential privacy is the only technique with formal protection against differencing, but it cannot rescue small cohorts. For a bounded mean, noise scales roughly with range/(n·ε), so at n of 5–50 and a strong ε near 1, the noise is as large as the real spread between people (an inference from standard DP mechanics). Budgets also add up across releases. Apple's deployment allowed **ε=16 per day**, which researchers called far above what the academic literature considers reasonable ([Tang, Korolova et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1709.02753)). Nightly recomputation is therefore off the table. Legally, pooling contributions is processing of special-category health data. Under GDPR that realistically requires explicit, granular, withdrawable Art. 9 consent, and the operator holding the source rows still holds personal data even if the published output is anonymous ([WP29](https://ec.europa.eu/justice/article-29/documentation/opinion-recommendation/files/2014/wp216_en.pdf); [ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/data-sharing/anonymisation/how-do-we-ensure-anonymisation-is-effective/)). Cross-instance pooling is the only version that could reach a meaningful n. It would need explicit per-metric consent, DP with a fixed lifetime budget, coarse bands, minimum-n suppression on top of DP, and an infrequent fixed release schedule. No precedent was found for federated DP health benchmarks across self-hosted open-source deployments, so this would be a research project, not a feature.

## Conclusion

The question changes once the evidence is laid out. It stops being "can we show percentiles?" and becomes "which reference is honest for this specific measurement?" The answer is metric by metric. For wearable-native metrics, the honest comparison is usually the user's own baseline. For manual measurements (grip, waist, pulse taken properly), it is a public-domain NHANES table the project derives and versions itself. In openhealth's case the data-quality ranking and the harm ranking run in opposite directions: body-size metrics have the best tables and the strongest harm signal, while HRV is the metric users most want ranked and has no defensible table. That argues for treating benchmarks as provenance-tagged reference data, recording source, cycle, method, population and license alongside each band, rather than as a generic "compare" feature. That fits the repo's existing emphasis on provenance and confidence signals. The largest unknown is behavioural: no trial has tested percentile-for-age displays in adults. An opt-in, self-trend-first design whose users can turn off comparisons is both the cautious choice and a way to learn whether rank helps anyone.
