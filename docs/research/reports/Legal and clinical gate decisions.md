# Replace every review gate with sourced defaults

> **Not legal or medical advice.** This is a sourced assessment written in place of professional review, because the project has decided to involve no lawyer or clinician. Each decision rests on the primary sources cited next to it and on the conservative reading of them. Items marked **[UNVERIFIED]** rely on text that was not fetched or confirmed during research and must be checked against the source before anyone quotes or relies on them. Items marked **[PRODUCT CHOICE]** are engineering or product decisions that no published source backs. They were chosen to be conservative and can be changed without contradicting any source.

Every decision that was previously gated on "counsel", "clinical review" or "ED-specialist review" can be closed with a concrete default. Primary sources support most of them directly. The rest are precautionary product choices, labelled as such. Four decisions matter most. **First, rename the product before public launch.** An AGPL-3.0 "OpenHealth" AI health assistant with about 4,000 GitHub stars, App Store sellers called "OpenHealth Inc" and "OpenHealth SRL", and the UK group OPEN Health, which claims a trademark in the health field, make "openhealth" unsafe as a public name. **Second, commit a GPLv3/AGPLv3 §7 app-store permission before the first external pull request.** Under DCO with no CLA, the maintainer cannot add it later for code they do not own. **Third, define the regulatory position through intended purpose and wording.** The app is a record-keeping and general-wellness tool that never flags values as abnormal, never computes risk, and never uses labs to decide eligibility for screening. The MDR has no open-source or free-service carve-out, so price and licence do not protect the app; its stated purpose does. **Fourth, fix the nutrition safety parameters in versioned config.** Calorie floors are **1,200 kcal/d for women and 1,500 kcal/d for men or undeclared sex** (AHA/ACC/TOS 2013). Automated deficits default to **min(500 kcal/d, 20% of TDEE)** with a hard cap of **750 kcal/d or 25%**, and projected loss of **0.7%/week by default and 1.0%/week at most**. There are no deficit proposals below **BMI 18.5**, current or projected. A fixed list of self-declared exclusions turns deficits off. The hosted service run by the maintainer is where nearly all of the legal exposure sits. It makes the maintainer a full GDPR controller of Article 9 health data for EU and US users alike, puts it in scope of the FTC Health Breach Notification Rule on paper, and makes MDR "commercial activity" arguable. The decisions below therefore apply most strictly to that deployment.

## Licensing and naming: three decisions must land before the first contributor

### 1. App-store licence exception

Reference it from `LICENSE`, `CONTRIBUTING.md` and the SPDX header of every file. Add this line to `CONTRIBUTING.md`: _"By signing off (DCO) you license your contribution under AGPL-3.0-or-later together with the additional permission in LICENSE-APP-STORE."_ Link the source repository from inside the app and from each store listing. Audit the mobile dependency tree. Third-party GPL or AGPL code without an equivalent permission cannot ship in the store build.

> **Additional permission under GNU AGPL version 3 section 7 (application store distribution)**
>
> As an additional permission under section 7 of the GNU Affero General Public License version 3, the copyright holders of openhealth grant you permission to convey the Program, or a work based on the Program, in object code form through an application distribution platform (including without limitation the Apple App Store, Google Play, and comparable services), notwithstanding that the terms of service, usage rules, or technical measures of such a platform impose restrictions on recipients' exercise of the rights granted by this License, including (a) limits on redistribution or further conveyance of the conveyed copy, (b) limits on the number of devices or accounts on which it may be installed or used, (c) a requirement that recipients accept the platform operator's terms, and (d) technical measures that control installation or execution of the conveyed copy;

>
> This permission applies only to conveyance through such platforms. It covers only material whose copyright holders have granted it. It does not and cannot grant permission for separately licensed components. As provided in section 7, you may remove this additional permission from any copy of the Program, or any part of it, that you convey.

Rename the product before release and replace "openhealth" in this text (see item 3).

**Basis.** The FSF's position is that App Store usage rules are **"exactly the kind of 'further restrictions' that are prohibited by the GPL"**, and Apple removed GNU Go rather than change its terms ([FSF](https://www.fsf.org/blogs/licensing/more-about-the-app-store-gpl-enforcement)). The GPL FAQ says exceptions are made "by granting an additional permission under section 7" ([GPL FAQ](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/gpl-faq.html#GPLIncompatibleLibs)). Signal, Element X, Threema and Immich ship AGPL apps with no store exception ([Signal-iOS LICENSE](https://github.com/signalapp/Signal-iOS/blob/HEAD/LICENSE); [element-x-ios LICENSE](https://github.com/element-hq/element-x-ios/blob/HEAD/LICENSE)). They can do this only because they control the copyright. Under DCO the maintainer does not, because the sign-off covers only "the open source license indicated in the file" ([DCO 1.1](https://developercertificate.org/)). The proposed text merges three existing permissions: SonoBus's channel condition ([SonoBus](https://github.com/sonosaurus/sonobus/blob/HEAD/LICENSE_EXCEPTION)), stardroid's enumerated restrictions ([stardroid](https://github.com/sky-map-team/stardroid/blob/HEAD/stardroid-v2/LICENSE.md)) and libsignal_dart's AGPL heading and scope clause ([libsignal_dart](https://github.com/djx-y-z/libsignal_dart/blob/HEAD/LICENSE.appstore)). The cost of leaving it out shows in Rust libsignal. It is AGPL with no such text, and downstream apps report that this blocks them ([libsignal#684](https://github.com/signalapp/libsignal/issues/684)), even though Signal's 2016 blog post said GPL apps were "explicitly authorized" for the App Store ([Signal blog](https://signal.org/blog/license-update/)). A blog post does not substitute for licence text. **[UNVERIFIED]:** whether SPDX has an `AdditionRef-` syntax that fits this text (use a `LicenseRef-` identifier or a plain pointer until checked), and whether the current Apple Media Services Terms still contain the "Usage Rules" clause the FSF quoted in 2010.

**Residual risk.** Forks may strip the permission, as §7 allows, and code a contributor submitted before the file existed is not covered. Both risks are low if the file is committed first.

### 2. Trademark policy

**Decision.** Publish a one-page `TRADEMARK.md` adapted from Matomo's CC-BY-SA policy, with attribution, and add a one-line pointer to it in `LICENSE`. The policy has six parts. _Scope:_ the word mark and logo; the AGPL grants no trademark rights. _Free use:_ truthful references ("works with X", "X integration"), redistribution of **unmodified** official releases under the same name, community, press and educational use, and a "Powered by X" link on self-hosted instances. _Must rename:_ any modified build or fork; any store build not made from an official tag; any hosted, multi-tenant or paid service offered to others (which may say "based on X"); and app names, package IDs and domains containing the mark. _Ask first:_ logos outside the unmodified app, merchandise, event names. _Never:_ implied endorsement, or medical or clinical claims attributed to the project. _Contact:_ an email address, and a statement that the project will ask politely before enforcing.

**Basis.** Matomo's policy separates permitted, non-commercial, unmodified use from restricted commercial, modified and domain use. It is "published under the CC-BY-SA license, and based on the Ubuntu project's trademark policy" ([Matomo](https://matomo.org/trademark/)). Bitwarden's licence likewise grants no trademark rights ([bitwarden/server LICENSE](https://github.com/bitwarden/server/blob/main/LICENSE.txt)). The trademark is what keeps the hosted brand distinct from forks. **[UNVERIFIED]:** the exact AGPLv3 §7(e) wording ("Declining to grant rights under trademark law…"), which was not fetched, and the Model Trademark Guidelines, whose site failed TLS.

**Residual risk.** Without a registration, the policy is enforceable only through weak unregistered-mark rights in Portugal and the EU. That is acceptable before launch.

### 3. Name clearance for "openhealth"

**Decision.** **Rename before any public release, store listing or domain purchase**, and keep "openhealth" as an internal codename only. The replacement should be a coined or arbitrary word in which "open", "health", "med", "care" and "vital" are not the dominant element. Before adopting it, run these knockout searches and record screenshots with dates in the decision log: TMview (EUIPO, INPI and national offices) in Nice classes 9, 10, 35, 42 and 44; EUIPO eSearch; the WIPO Global Brand Database; USPTO tmsearch; INPI Portugal; App Store names through the iTunes Search API; Google Play; GitHub, npm and PyPI; .com, .app, .org and .pt domains; and a negative-meaning check in PT, EN, ES, FR and DE. If the name is clean, consider an EUTM filing in classes 9, 42 and 44 (fee about €850 for one class, **[UNVERIFIED]**).

**Basis.** The collision is direct. **OpenHealthForAll/open-health is an AGPL-3.0 "OpenHealth, AI Health Assistant" with 3,959 stars** that uses "your personal health information" ([GitHub](https://github.com/OpenHealthForAll/open-health)). The App Store lists "OpenHealth: Self-help Loops" and the sellers "OpenHealth Inc" (Plates: AI Calorie Tracker) and "OpenHealth SRL" (DataMed, Medical) (iTunes Search API, PT and US storefronts, queried 2026-10-02). EUIPO, WIPO and INPI could not be queried automatically, so whether an EUTM exists is **[UNVERIFIED]**. The decision to rename does not depend on that answer.

**Residual risk.** Whatever the new name's knockout search misses. Keeping "openhealth" would mean near-certain confusion and SEO collision, and a plausible complaint from OPEN Health.

### 4. Open Food Facts and ODbL

**Decision.** Store OFF data in a dedicated `off_products` table carrying OFF code, last-modified date and import batch. Keep it separate from `fdc_foods` and from user tables, combine them at query time through a view with a `source` column, and never merge them into one canonical foods table. Treat any filtered or normalised OFF table as a **Derivative Database**. Publish the import and transform scripts and declare the OFF-derived tables ODbL in `DATA-LICENSES.md`, which satisfies the §4.6(b) "method of making the alterations" requirement. Show _"Contains data from Open Food Facts, available under the Open Database License"_, with both names linked, on every OFF-backed product view and search result, in About and Settings, and in export footers. Do not store product images, or store them only with per-image CC BY-SA credit. Use the nightly dumps for bulk data and the API only for real user scans, with an identifying User-Agent. Put user corrections in an overlay table and offer to push them back to OFF. Food-log exports that contain OFF-derived nutrient values carry the ODbL notice.

**Basis.** ODbL 1.0 states that an unmodified database "as part of a collection of independent databases" is a Collective Database, "not … a Derivative Database", and that the collective need not be licensed under ODbL (§4.5a). Any "modification, or any other alteration" is a derivative. Using the database to create a Produced Work "does not create a Derivative Database" (§4.5b). If a Derivative Database or a Produced Work from one is Publicly Used, the derivative or "the method of making the alterations" must be offered (§4.4c, §4.6) ([ODbL 1.0](https://opendatacommons.org/licenses/odbl/1-0/)). Displaying data to tenants of a hosted service is Public Use, because "Publicly" means anyone outside the licensee's control. OFF asks for attribution "on product pages, search screens, and in the 'About' or 'Settings' sections", sets the rule "one call = one real scan", and asks apps to send contributions back ([OFF data](https://world.openfoodfacts.org/data)). Its images are CC BY-SA ([OFF terms](https://world.openfoodfacts.org/terms-of-use)). By analogy, OSMF's guideline says a result "intended for the extraction of the original data" is a database, not a Produced Work ([OSMF](https://osmfoundation.org/wiki/Licence/Community_Guidelines/Produced_Work_-_Guideline)). Per-user logs are therefore Produced Works. A bulk "export my food database" feature is not.

**Residual risk.** Low. The failure modes are missing attribution or a quietly merged proprietary foods table, and both can be checked in code review.

### 5. Recipe URL import

**Decision.** Fetch only when the user submits a URL. There is no crawling, prefetching or discovery. Parse schema.org `Recipe` JSON-LD and store parsed `recipeIngredient` entries, `recipeInstructions` as an editable user draft, servings, times, `source_url`, site name, author credit and `imported_at`. Never store images, headnotes, stories or reviews; link to the original instead. Imported recipes are private to the user's organisation. Public sharing is off by default and requires the user to rewrite the steps and keep attribution. Limit each user to 10 imports per minute and add a global cap per domain. Send an honest User-Agent and honour robots.txt `Disallow`. Harden against SSRF: http and https only; block private, loopback, link-local and metadata ranges and re-check them after each redirect; at most 3 redirects, a 10-second timeout and a 2 MB cap; `text/html` only; no cookies or auth; isolated egress; no logging of page bodies. Provide a takedown contact. On a request, delete the imported text and keep only the link. The rate limits and size caps are **[PRODUCT CHOICE]**.

**Basis.** US Copyright Office Circular 33 says "a mere listing of ingredients or contents, or a simple set of directions, is uncopyrightable", and that registration does not cover "the list of ingredients… [or] the underlying process" ([Circular 33](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ33.pdf)). The Seventh Circuit held that ingredient lists "lack the requisite element of originality" ([Publications Int'l v. Meredith, 88 F.3d 473](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/721354/publications-international-limited-plaintiffcounterdefendant-appellant/)). Portugal's CDADC Art. 1(2) excludes "processos… métodos operacionais" (processes and operating methods) from protection ([CDADC](https://www.gda.pt/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/codigododireitodeautorcdadclei162008.pdf)). **[UNVERIFIED]:** the EU points, which come from background knowledge and were not fetched. They are the lawful-user right to extract insubstantial parts under Database Directive Art. 8(1), the bar on repeated systematic extraction in Art. 7(5) ([96/9/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:31996L0009)), _Ryanair v PR Aviation_ (C-30/14) on contractual restrictions in site terms, and the DSM Art. 4 machine-readable opt-out ([2019/790](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2019/790/oj)).

**Residual risk.** Low. The main exposure is a site's terms of service forbidding automated access, which is a contract claim. User-initiated fetching, rate limits and the takedown path address it.

### 6. Licensing of preventive-guideline facts

**Decision.** For each guideline, store only structured facts the project writes itself: `source_org`, `source_id`/version, the authority's short title, `grade`, a structured `population` (sex, age_min, age_max, risk flags), `interval`, `effective_date`, `canonical_url`, `retrieved_at` and `content_hash`. Do not store recommendation text, rationale or clinical-consideration text. Display these fields as labelled data with a deep link ("Read the recommendation at <authority>"), not as rewritten prose sentences. This keeps the recommendation's meaning unchanged without copying protected text. Apply these rules per source. **USPSTF:** facts and a link, the citation "Source: USPSTF, [topic], [date], [URL]", and "Not endorsed by AHRQ/HHS/USPSTF". Apply for approval to use the Prevention TaskForce API, which is a licence application, not a legal review. **NICE:** link only, with no stored NICE-derived fields. **WHO:** facts and a link, no text or tables, never the logo. **DGS:** facts, a link and the citation "Norma DGS n.º X/AAAA". **All sources:** a UI line stating that the source organisations do not endorse the app.

**Basis.** AHRQ allows USPSTF work to be reproduced only "without any changes", forbids its incorporation "into a profit-making venture without the express written permission of AHRQ", and says AHRQ "cannot endorse, or appear to endorse, derivative or excerpted materials" ([USPSTF copyright](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/index.php/recommendation-topics/copyright-notice)). NICE requires "written permission… subject to a fee" for use "as part of a service… application or product available outside the UK", and states "Web scraping of our content… is not permitted" ([NICE international](https://www.nice.org.uk/reusing-our-content/use-of-our-content-internationally)). A worldwide service licence costs £60,000 a year ([NICE reuse](https://www.nice.org.uk/re-using-our-content)). WHO publications are CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 IGO and "cannot be used to promote or endorse products" ([WHO copyright](https://www.who.int/about/policies/publishing/copyright)). CDADC Art. 1(2) leaves methods and concepts unprotected ([CDADC](https://www.gda.pt/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/codigododireitodeautorcdadclei162008.pdf)). **[UNVERIFIED]:** DGS's own reuse terms, which were not found, and whether DGS _Normas_ count as unprotected official texts under CDADC Art. 8.

**Residual risk.** Low for USPSTF, WHO and DGS facts with links. Medium for NICE, so it stays link-only.

The ~1,000-user DPO trigger is a **[PRODUCT CHOICE]**, because Art. 37 sets no numeric "large scale" threshold.

**Residual risk.** No court has decided whether a small friends-only D2 deployment is "purely" household, and "large scale" has no number. The conservative defaults treat both as in scope.

### 8. FTC Health Breach Notification Rule

**Decision.** If any US resident can sign up, D3 complies by design. Never send health data to advertising, analytics or attribution SDKs. Name every sub-processor in the privacy notice and gate each one behind explicit opt-in. Keep a breach log with discovery timestamps. Keep a notice template that meets the § 318.6 content and "Clear and Conspicuous" rules, sent by email plus an in-app banner. Run one incident clock: CNPD within 72 hours; individuals and the FTC within 60 days at the latest; FTC notice at the same time as individual notice when 500 or more people are affected. Encrypt data with separately stored keys. Notify all affected users rather than trying to determine US residency. Do not geo-block as a substitute, because the rule follows residency, not location. P is not a vendor. The self-hosting guide tells D2 operators serving US residents that they are plausibly vendors. D1 is treated as unknown and negligible.

**Basis.** The rule "applies to foreign and domestic vendors… irrespective of any jurisdictional tests in the… FTC Act, that maintain information of U.S. citizens or residents" (§ 318.1). Its "health care services or supplies" definition includes apps that track "fitness… sleep… diet". A personal health record is one with "the technical capacity to draw information from multiple sources". The rule's own Example 1 is a diet and fitness app that syncs a wearable, and a "breach" includes "unauthorized disclosure" (§ 318.2). Notice is due "in no case later than 60 calendar days after the discovery" ([89 FR 47028](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule); [eCFR Part 318](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318)). Penalties reach **$53,088 per violation** ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-ftcs-health-breach-notification-rule-0)). In the GoodRx and Easy Healthcare orders, disclosures inconsistent with privacy promises counted as unauthorized ([89 FR 47028](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule)). HIPAA does not apply to an app the individual chose that acts for no covered entity ([HHS FAQ 3013](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/3013/does-hipaa-require-a-covered-entity-to-enter-into-a-business-associate-agreement.html)).

**Residual risk.** Enforcement against an EU-resident individual is unlikely but legally claimed. The practical exposure is reputational and comes through app-store and partner requirements.

### 9. EU MDR and FDA scope, and the intended-purpose statement

**Decision.** Publish this statement, word for word, in the README, on the landing page, in the in-app About screen and in every store listing:

> "openhealth is a personal record-keeping and general-wellness app. It helps you store, organise and view your own health-related records (such as lab results you enter or import, body measurements, workouts, nutrition, sleep and wearable data) and track healthy habits. It shows publicly available guidance published by recognised public-health authorities, attributed and unaltered, for your information. openhealth is not a medical device. It is not intended to diagnose, screen for, prevent, monitor, predict, treat or alleviate any disease or condition, and it does not provide medical advice. Talk to a qualified healthcare professional about your health."

Adopt this **never-add list**. Adding any item requires reopening this decision.

1. Flagging lab values as "abnormal", "high" or "low", or applying reference ranges or colour-coded risk. Showing the lab's own printed range verbatim as stored data is the limit.
2. Any risk score or calculator (ASCVD, FINDRISC, FRAX, "biological age" presented as health risk, disease probability).
3. Screening eligibility or urgency logic that uses labs, vitals or diagnoses. Only age, sex and user-declared criteria taken from the authority may be used.
4. Naming a disease in an alert ("you may have prediabetes"), or prompting clinical action ("see a doctor about your blood pressure", "adjust medication").
5. Medication dosing, titration, interaction checking, or adherence features framed as treatment. A plain user-set reminder is the limit.
6. AI output that interprets results diagnostically, triages, or recommends treatment.
7. Claims such as "clinical-grade", "medically accurate", "doctor-approved", "detects", "screens", "prevents [disease]" or "early warning".
8. Arrhythmia, AFib, ECG, SpO2 or blood-pressure disease alerts, or continuous monitoring of a condition.
9. Clinician-facing decision dashboards with interpretation.
10. Positioning the app as managing a named chronic disease.

Use FDA category-2 phrasing ("may help reduce the risk of") only when quoting an authority, and never in EU copy. Forecasting features must not project clinical markers such as HbA1c.

**Basis.** MDR Art. 2(1) makes software a device when "intended by the manufacturer" for "diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, prediction, prognosis, treatment". Art. 2(12) defines intended purpose by "the label… instructions for use… promotional or sales materials or statements". Art. 2(27) covers supply "in the course of a commercial activity, whether in return for payment or free of charge". Art. 6(2) reaches free online "diagnostic or therapeutic service[s]" ([MDR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2017/745/oj)). The Blue Guide treats commercial activity as a "business related context", excludes "occasional supplies by… hobbyists", and says nothing about open source ([Blue Guide 2022](<https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:52022XC0629(04)>)). The CRA's "not monetised" FOSS carve-out applies only by analogy ([CRA](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/2847/oj)), so **intended purpose, not price or licence, is the real defence for D3**. MDCG 2019-11 rev.1 says wellness and fitness apps "do not qualify as MDSW", nor does software limited to "storage, archival, communication, simple search" or "generic… pathways (not directed to individual patients)". It places software that prevents "the risk of illnesses… by analysing physiological parameters" in **class IIa** ([MDCG 2019-11 rev.1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)). The FDA's January 2026 General Wellness guidance covers apps that "log, track, or trend" activity, sleep and food. It allows "values, ranges, trends, baselines". It excludes products with "references to specific diseases… or diagnostic thresholds", "alerts… that recommend… specific clinical action", or claims of "clinical accuracy, medical or clinical grade" ([FDA General Wellness](https://www.fda.gov/media/90652/download)). **[UNVERIFIED]:** whether the FDA's 2022 Device Software Functions guidance has been updated, and how the EU AI Act applies to the AI coach.

**Residual risk.** Reminders filtered by age and sex are an unadjudicated grey zone under MDCG step 4. Keep them as attributed listings with no urgency language.

This applies to the README, landing page, app, store listings and social posts. Enforce it with a PR checklist item and a CI grep over the banned terms. The AI coach gets its own system-prompt guardrails and a CI suite of refusal tests, because a grep cannot catch claims implied in generated text.

### 11. Governing preventive-care rules without a clinician

**Decision.** Adopt **Source-Fidelity Governance** as an ADR in place of DCB0129-style clinical safety. It has nine parts.

1. **Allowed sources** are public authorities whose terms permit the use, recorded in a `sources` registry with URL, licence, retrieval date and hash. USPSTF qualifies through the approved API. DGS qualifies for facts and a link. NICE and WHO are limited as described in item 6. CDC/ACIP, ECDC and NHS syndication stay link-only until the maintainer has read their terms **[UNVERIFIED]**.
2. **Fidelity:** the fields from item 6 are shown exactly as the authority states them, each authority separately, never merged or harmonised.
3. **Deterministic filtering** uses only the authority's own fields (age band, sex, user-declared criteria), never labs or vitals, and is unit-tested against fixtures built from the authority's data.
4. **Expiry:** each item carries `retrieved_at` and `review_by`, at most 90 days, or weekly for USPSTF. Expired items show "may be out of date – check source". An upstream diff blocks changed items until they are re-approved.
5. **Logged two-step review:** an automated hash diff, then the maintainer confirms field match, attribution and licence. This is a source-fidelity check, not a clinical judgement.
6. **Framing on every item:** _"Information from <Authority>, published <date>. This is general public-health guidance, not personal medical advice. It may not apply to you. Discuss with a healthcare professional."_ Never "overdue" or "you need".
7. **A non-clinical hazard log**, reviewed each release. Examples: wrong age calculation, stale guidance, wrong-sex match, reminder timezone errors, AI hallucination.
8. **A "Report a problem with this guidance" channel**, with a set window for pulling faulty items.
9. **The AI coach** cites only stored authority items and the user's own data, and refuses diagnosis, treatment and risk interpretation.

**Basis.** No source found requires a named clinician for non-device wellness software. The FDA itself treats "official statements made by healthcare professional organizations" as an adequate evidence anchor ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/media/90652/download)). MDCG excludes "generic… pathways (not directed to individual patients)" ([MDCG 2019-11 rev.1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)). USPSTF runs an API that filters "according to… age, sex, selected behavioral risk factors", requires prior approval, and recommends a weekly refresh ([USPSTF API](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/apps/api.jsp)). This is an authority-sanctioned version of the planned feature. The NHS syndication terms require adaptations that "must not materially change the meaning" and that warnings be kept ([NHS developer hub](https://digital.nhs.uk/developer)); that text came from a search summary and is **[UNVERIFIED]**. That DCB0129 and DTAC bind only health IT deployed in the NHS is an inference **[UNVERIFIED]**.

**Residual risk.** Nobody independently checks whether guidance fits a particular user. The policy never asserts that it does.

## Nutrition safety: guideline ranges become hard ceilings

The numbers below come mainly from clinical obesity programmes with professional contact [CLINICAL] and from supervised athlete trials [ATHLETE]. In an unsupervised app they are used as **ceilings on how aggressive a proposal can be, never as targets shown to the user**. Every value lives in versioned config with its source. Every adaptive proposal requires the user to accept it.

### 12. Calorie floor

**Decision.** Effective floor = **max(estimated RMR, sex floor)**. The sex floor is **1,200 kcal/d for women and 1,500 kcal/d for men or users who have not declared sex**. Never propose anything below the floor. The floor value is never shown as a goal, and refusal copy never states it.

**Basis.** AHA/ACC/TOS 2013 Recommendation 3a says "Prescribe 1200–1500 kcal/d for women and 1500–1800 kcal/d for men" ([Jensen et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5819889/)). NICE NG246 limits 800–1,200 kcal/d "low-energy diets" to "a specialist overweight and obesity management service" and under 800 kcal/d to clinically assessed need (1.16.8–1.16.9) ([NICE NG246](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng246/chapter/Physical-activity-and-diet)). The female floor therefore sits at the lowest value both sources allow. Using 1,500 kcal/d for undeclared sex and including the RMR term are **[PRODUCT CHOICE]**s. No guideline says intake must stay above RMR, and RMR equations carry about ±10% individual error. The NHLBI 1998 ranges are **[UNVERIFIED]** and are not relied on.

**Residual risk.** 1,200 kcal/d is a boundary value with no margin for small women. The RMR term usually lifts the floor above it.

### 13. Deficit cap

**Decision.** An automated proposal's deficit is at most **min(500 kcal/d, 20% of estimated TDEE, the deficit implied by the rate cap in item 14)**. The user may raise it to at most **750 kcal/d or 25% of TDEE**, whichever is lower; the AHA's 30% figure is never used.

**Basis.** AHA prescribes "a 500-kcal/d or 750-kcal/d energy deficit", and its evidence statement also allows a 30% deficit, all in supervised programmes ([Jensen et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5819889/)). In Garthe 2011, the slow arm cut intake by **19%** and gained lean mass. The fast arm cut **30%** and saw no lean-mass gain ([Garthe et al. 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21558571/)). The 20% cap rounds Garthe's slow arm. The 7,700 kcal/kg conversion used to link rate and deficit is a standard approximation and is unsourced here. The earlier evidence notes that static energy equivalents over-project early loss ([Hall](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17848938/)).

**Residual risk.** TDEE is itself an estimate, so the real deficit can be larger than 20%. The floor still holds.

### 14. Rate cap

**Decision.** Projected loss defaults to **0.7% of bodyweight per week**. The user may choose up to **1.0%/week**, and no faster option exists. Average the rate from multiple weigh-ins.

**Basis.** Garthe recommends "a weekly BW loss of 0.7%" ([Garthe 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21558571/)). Helms gives "approximately 0.5 to 1%/wk" and warns of eating-disorder risk ([Helms et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24864135/)). Both studied athletes under supervision, which is an optimistic population. The conservative choice is the bottom of Helms's range as the default and Garthe's recommended rate as the ceiling. Iraki supports averaging weekly weight from daily weigh-ins ([Iraki et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710/)).

**Residual risk.** No trial covers unsupervised general-population app users.

### 15. Week-to-week step change and surplus

**Decision.** Each accepted target change is at most **200 kcal or 10% of the current target**, whichever is smaller. This is a **[PRODUCT CHOICE]**, because no source sets a step limit. Gain proposals default to **+10% of TDEE**, at most **+20%**, aiming at **≤0.5% of bodyweight per week**.

**Basis.** For novices, Iraki recommends a "hyper-energetic diet (~10–20%)… with a target weight gain of ~0.25–0.5% of bodyweight/week" ([Iraki et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710/)).

**Residual risk.** The step limit slows convergence when the estimate is noisy. That is the intent.

### 16. BMI gate

**Decision.** Make **no deficit proposal when current BMI is under 18.5, or when projected end-of-plan BMI would fall under 18.5**. Deficit proposals only follow a weight-loss goal the user set, and each one needs explicit acceptance. Hide-numbers mode enforces the gate silently, and the refusal never states BMI or the threshold.

**Basis.** WHO defines adult underweight as "BMI < 18.5" ([WHO GHO](https://www.who.int/data/gho/data/themes/topics/topic-details/GHO/body-mass-index)). AHA and NICE address diet prescription to people "who would benefit from weight loss" or are "living with overweight/obesity" ([Jensen et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5819889/); [NICE NG246](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng246/chapter/Physical-activity-and-diet)). No guideline-grade source supports a cutoff of 20.

**Residual risk.** BMI misclassifies very muscular people and older adults.

### 19. Nutrient reference values

**Decision.** EU, EEA and UK users default to **EFSA DRVs**, credited "Source: EFSA". US users default to **NASEM DRIs**, stored only as per-nutrient numbers in the project's own schema with a citation to the NASEM report and a link to the NIH ODS fact sheet. Never copy NASEM tables or text. The schema carries `nutrient_code`, `value`, `unit`, `reference_type` (with EFSA AR/PRI/AI/RI/UL and NASEM EAR/RDA/AI/UL/AMDR/CDRR as **distinct enums**), `population_group`, `basis`, `source_org`, `source_document`, `source_version_or_year`, `retrieved_at`, `region_default` and `licence_note`. Never label a user "deficient".

**Basis.** EFSA states "Re-use is authorised, provided that EFSA is acknowledged as the source" ([EFSA legal notice](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/legalnotice)). NIH content is "in the public domain and can be used without charge" ([NIH FAQ](https://www.nih.gov/about-nih/frequently-asked-questions)). NASEM publications are "© 2026 National Academy of Sciences. All Rights Reserved" ([NAP 11537](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/11537/dietary-reference-intakes-the-essential-guide-to-nutrient-requirements)). **[UNVERIFIED]:** whether the EFSA DRV Finder offers a bulk download with additional terms.

**Residual risk.** Bundling NASEM numbers relies on bare facts being uncopyrightable, which is the standard US position, applied conservatively here.

### 20. Protein defaults

**Decision.** The baseline suggestion is **0.8 g/kg/day**. Users who opt in as active or resistance-training get a range of **1.2–1.6 g/kg/day**, worded with a caveat. Users aged 65 or over get **1.0–1.2 g/kg/day**. Users who declare CKD are capped at **0.8 g/kg/day** with a note to talk to their care team, and never see a higher suggestion. Nobody is defaulted to 2.3–3.1 g/kg.

**Basis.** The ISSN gives "1.4-2.0 g protein/kg body weight/day… for most exercising individuals", and 2.3–3.1 only for resistance-trained people during hypocaloric periods ([Jäger et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/)). A meta-analysis found fat-free-mass gains plateau around **1.62 g/kg/day** ([Morton et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608)). PROT-AGE and KDIGO are cited under item 17. The 1.2–1.6 band is a **[PRODUCT CHOICE]**: a conservative band that tops out at Morton's plateau and starts below the ISSN range. **[UNVERIFIED this pass]:** the US RDA of 0.8 g/kg (NASEM Macronutrients report) and the EFSA PRI of 0.83 g/kg, both well established but not re-fetched.

**Residual risk.** CKD is often undiagnosed, so the opt-in higher range carries a caveat rather than a guarantee.

## Conclusion

Taken together, the gates were about two different things. The legal gates turn out to be mostly about **timing and wording, not judgement**. The app-store permission, the rename and the trademark policy are cheap now and close to impossible later. The MDR, FDA and UCPD positions all turn on what the project says about itself, so one intended-purpose statement, a banned-word list and a never-add list replace the open-ended "legal review" with something CI can check. The clinical gates had a different problem. The guidelines cover clinical populations with professional contact, so applying them to an unsupervised app means using their **lower bounds as hard ceilings** and adding self-declared exclusions on top. The precautionary items (breastfeeding, the RMR term, step limits, the BMI ≥25 rule) are where a clinician's input would have changed the most. Labelling them as product choices keeps them open to revision without implying that evidence backs them.

The work that remains is verification, not review. Re-fetch the items marked [UNVERIFIED] (the 988 line, the RDA and PRI quotes, the ADA §8 text, the AGPLv3 §7(e) wording, SPDX syntax, and the DGS and NHS terms), run the manual trademark searches for the new name, and re-verify the helpline numbers on a schedule. None of this needs a professional. It needs a dated log, which the Source-Fidelity Governance ADR already provides.
