# Collect less; validate scores before implementation

**OpenValens should collect inputs for a specific feature, not require a comprehensive health profile before providing value.** The reviewed papers support selected measurements, some behavior-change interventions, and construct-specific questionnaires; they do not validate OpenValens’s unspecified daily-readiness composite. Preserve the preference for two numbers—daily readiness and longer-term personal-goal progress—but distinguish an experimental readiness number from transparent goal arithmetic and from a validated health measure. Make goals editable, check-ins optional, and missing information explicit; these are defensible design recommendations, not proven retention interventions. Keep manual/no-wearable access where the underlying information can be meaningfully recorded, without claiming sensor-equivalent scores. Before committing #98, #92, or #95, settle the intended population, permissible claims, and validation path rather than accepting earlier speculative formulas.

## Onboarding needs a purpose for every field

The strongest justification for demographic collection here is **feature-specific calculation**, not generic personalization. Mifflin et al. derived a resting-energy prediction equation using weight, height, age, and a binary sex-coded term in healthy adults. That establishes inputs to that model, not individualized calorie requirements, a total-energy prescription, or applicability to every adult. Its derivation population does not establish pediatric validity or a mapping for gender identity, intersex traits, or hormone treatment ([Mifflin](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2305711/)).

The following collection policy is a **product inference constrained by evidence**, not an experimentally validated onboarding flow.

| Information                                 | Purpose and collection boundary                                                                                                                                                                                  | Unknown-state behavior                                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Age or eligibility band                     | Establish an adult-only policy if chosen; request age in years only when a calculation needs it. These purposes do not require exact birth date.                                                                 | Do not infer age; withhold dependent estimates or eligibility-dependent features.           |
| Height and dated weight, with units         | Request for an explicitly selected anthropometric or energy-estimation feature, not ordinary logging, education, or unrelated goals. BMI is not a body-composition measurement.                                  | Do not insert defaults; preserve source and date.                                           |
| Equation-specific sex variable              | Consider only at the point of a supported calculation, with the model’s historical variable and applicability explained. Do not substitute gender identity or presume anatomy determines the coefficient.        | Allow uncertainty/declining; withhold an unsupported estimate rather than force assignment. |
| Activity category and weight-change goal    | Collect only if a separately reviewed feature requires them. An activity multiplier introduces assumptions; “cut,” “bulk,” and “recomp” are preferences, not physiological measurements. Offer non-weight goals. | No inferred goal or calorie prescription from profile completion.                           |
| Pregnancy, medications, smoking, conditions | Do not require generic risk flags. Identify the actual clinical use, relevant guideline, jurisdiction, and safety owner before collecting them.                                                                  | Unknown, declined, not applicable, and “no” must remain distinct.                           |
| Goal, check-in, or connected measurement    | Request the metric, timeframe, and input method needed for the selected feature. Explain why a connection or answer helps; offer manual capture where meaningful.                                                | Missing observations are not zero activity, absence of symptoms, or poor readiness.         |

**Minimization is a privacy and product-design rationale, not evidence that fewer questions improve health.** The reviewed evidence does not establish a scientifically necessary universal profile bundle. Collecting sensitive flags also creates expectations of safe clinical interpretation that this research has not established. Decide geography, age eligibility, consent, and clinical scope separately. WHO’s pediatric BMI-for-age references demonstrate that age-specific interpretation exists; they do not authorize applying adult energy equations or adult score thresholds to children ([WHO](https://www.who.int/tools/growth-reference-data-for-5to19-years/indicators/bmi-for-age)).

The direct onboarding study is narrow: Koller et al.’s small, nonrandomized chronic-pain study found greater adoption with assisted setup, but no statistically significant difference in cumulative log-ins. It cannot prescribe a consumer onboarding sequence or demonstrate sustained benefit ([Koller](https://doi.org/10.2196/78827)). Optional setup, “not now,” save/resume, and progressive disclosure remain usability hypotheses. Test them instead of claiming an optimal screen count or completion time. Accessibility needs direct evaluation across keyboard, screen reader, navigation, comprehension, and pacing; an accessibility scoping review supports systematic attention but does not prove one flow improves retention ([Jonsson](https://doi.org/10.2196/45118)).

## Goals support agency; check-ins describe reported experience

**Personalization is not uniformly superior.** Nuijten et al.’s randomized workplace trial compared adaptive and generic goals in a gamified activity campaign. It found selective benefits for sports-session frequency and perceived biking performance, while use declined in both groups. Greater engagement among people who had stated a goal was an association, not a randomized test of requiring goal selection. The findings do not establish long-term health benefits or generalize to every goal domain ([Nuijten](https://doi.org/10.2196/28801)).

Let people choose, edit, defer, pause, or decline a goal. Specify its metric and timeframe, and explain whether a suggestion comes from recorded history, a named guideline, or an experimental rule. Preserve the preference for personal goals rather than silently substituting population norms. Start with goal-specific quantities: logged sessions against a chosen session target, for example. **This is transparent arithmetic over recorded data—not a health measure, physiological improvement estimate, or established predictive score.** If an overall progress number is desired, disclose its aggregation and missing-data behavior; combining domains introduces value judgments even when each calculation is simple.

Grady et al.’s meta-analysis found very-low-certainty evidence that engagement strategies improve digital-intervention use and user experience. It examined health-related secondary outcomes too, but does not establish efficacy for OpenValens’s particular goals, reminders, or scores ([Grady](https://doi.org/10.2196/47987)). Tracker interventions have broader evidence for increasing physical activity in clinical and nonclinical populations; that intervention evidence does not validate a particular tracker measurement or progress index ([Ferguson](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35868813/)). Evaluate adoption, meaningful use, retention, behavior, and health outcomes separately. Notification timing, frequency, pause controls, and check-in cadence need user research; this review establishes no optimal schedule.

For check-ins, distinguish **a report of how you feel** from a measurement of physiological readiness. Athlete research supports subjective monitoring of training response, but cannot establish general-adult daily norms or clinical interpretations ([Saw](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-094758)). Keep wording, scale, recall period, and version consistent; show answers and trends without assuming disagreement with a sensor is error. Custom brief questions can serve a descriptive purpose, but are not validated substitutes for standardized instruments.

PROMIS Fatigue has psychometric evidence for a specific fatigue construct. The Ameringer study evaluates **Fatigue 7a**, with past-week recall, across specified clinical samples and controls—not an instantaneous readiness questionnaire. Changing the recall period or combining its result with unrelated domains requires fresh justification. Confirm the exact instrument, interpretation, translation, and electronic-use permissions before adoption ([Ameringer](https://doi.org/10.1097/NNR.0000000000000162)).

## Measurement evidence does not validate the proposed composite

The evidence answers different questions. **Measurement validation** asks whether a device or questionnaire measures its stated quantity. **Associations** describe relationships without establishing causation. **Randomized interventions** test outcomes of an assigned strategy. **Guidelines and policies** provide practice or governance frameworks, not experimental proof of an app feature. None automatically validates a new composite constructed from those inputs.

| Claim supported by the source                                                                                                          | Evidence strength/type                                                                                           | Limitation for OpenValens                                                                                           |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Some tested wearables estimate nocturnal resting heart rate and HRV with useful agreement. [Dial](https://doi.org/10.14814/phy2.70527) | Direct ECG-reference measurement validation.                                                                     | Small healthy-adult sample; device/protocol-specific, not readiness or manual/wearable equivalence.                 |
| Consumer sleep-tracker performance differs by device and metric. [Lee](https://doi.org/10.2196/50983)                                  | Multicenter comparison against polysomnography.                                                                  | Tested models and clinical setting; sleep estimates are not diagnostic ground truth or a validated readiness scale. |
| Steps, heart rate, and energy expenditure have different accuracy profiles. [Fuller](https://doi.org/10.2196/18694)                    | Systematic review of measurement studies.                                                                        | Heterogeneous devices/settings and older models; do not generalize calorie accuracy from step accuracy.             |
| HRV-guided endurance training improved some submaximal physiological endpoints. [Düking](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34489178/)    | Small intervention meta-analysis; performance and peak oxygen-uptake effects were not statistically significant. | Endurance-training context, not general-adult daily readiness or a universally ineffective HRV signal.              |
| Acute:chronic workload ratios have reported injury associations in team sports. [Griffin](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-019-01218-2)  | Systematic review of observational associations.                                                                 | Sport-dependent definitions and methods; no causal or universal “safe zone” for general users.                      |

Preserve the metric definition, measurement window, source/device, timestamps, quality flags, and relevant algorithm version. Show personal trends within a reasonably consistent method; do not claim baselines cure measurement error or establish predictive validity. This review identifies **no justified universal baseline duration, confidence threshold, score weights, or cutoffs for OpenValens**. Missing inputs should not silently become average values or penalties. A manually reported sleep interval and a device-derived sleep estimate can both be useful without being interchangeable.

## Reconsider formula commitments without discarding product preferences

For **#98**, replace any universal demographic prerequisite with a purpose-based field inventory and explicit unavailable states. For **#92/#95**, retain two time horizons, explanations, eventual coverage of all four readiness and all five long-term domains, incremental releases, and full web/mobile parity as product commitments—not efficacy findings. Parity should include missing-data explanations and manual paths, not fabricated numeric equivalence. Domain coverage does not require every domain at account creation or one scientifically homogeneous scale.

Reopen any earlier commitments to fixed composite weights, vendor-score mappings, population-normalized “readiness,” calorie-derived certainty, injury thresholds, or equivalent manual/wearable scoring. Earlier speculative formulas are not evidence. **The reviewed sources do not validate our own unspecified composite; this is not a claim that no validated score exists anywhere.** Three paths remain: describe components without a readiness total; test a clearly labeled experimental numerical summary; or pursue a defined validated construct before making calibrated physiological or clinical claims. An experimental number preserves the preferred interface, but must not imply diagnostic, predictive, or treatment authority merely because it has explanations.

Before implementation, discuss four decisions: **Who is the product for?** Define general-adult versus clinical use and minor handling. **What does each number mean?** Choose descriptive/experimental readiness versus a validation program, and personal-goal arithmetic versus any health claim. **Which inputs earn collection?** Approve purpose-specific fields, check-in constructs, optional/manual routes, and unknown states. **What evidence gates release?** Agree on web/mobile accessibility and comprehension testing, instrument/device applicability, harms monitoring, and—if predictive claims are intended—prospective target-population and external validation. These decisions precede numerical weights or minimum-days rules.

## Conclusion

The immediate opportunity is to separate information people can use from authority the product has not earned. Purpose-specific onboarding, faithful self-report, and explainable goal arithmetic can provide value while a readiness hypothesis is investigated. Remaining research gaps include inclusive energy-estimation applicability, brief general-adult daily instruments, manual/passive comparability, domain-specific goal safety, and jurisdiction-specific clinical rules. They need explicit owners and additional research, not silent defaults.
