# Ship one hardened Compose path, nothing more

openhealth should copy the pattern that sustains Immich, Plausible and Paperless-ngx: one officially supported Docker Compose install, migrations that run automatically on boot, secrets validated at startup, a first-run owner claim that cannot be raced, no phone-home telemetry by default, and a backup, export and delete toolkit built into the product rather than left as homework. Two parts need more care than comparable apps give them, because openhealth stores health data. The first is the database port. Docker publishes ports past ufw, and bots wipe exposed Postgres servers within hours, so Postgres must never be published. The second is mobile push, which self-hosters cannot do without a project-run relay; that relay should carry only an opaque ID. The owner's licensing choices (AGPLv3 everywhere, DCO sign-off, no CLA) match the dominant precedent in this niche, but they have three consequences to plan for now. The maintainer's hosted service must publish its own modifications once outside contributions are merged. Proprietary hosted-only features must be separate programs. And the AGPL mobile app inherits Apple's history of conflict with the GPL, which a DCO does nothing to fix. The hosted service also changes the legal position. A pure open-source supplier is generally neither controller nor processor for self-hosted instances. A maintainer who runs hosted openhealth, a push relay or a telemetry endpoint "provides the means" under GDPR Recital 18, and a commercial hosted PHR likely falls under the FTC Health Breach Notification Rule. Items marked **[COUNSEL]** below need a lawyer before anyone relies on them; none of this is legal advice.

## AGPL without a CLA binds the maintainer's hosted service too

The owner's choice sits in the mainstream of self-hosted consumer software. **Immich, Nextcloud, Plausible, Mealie and wger are all AGPL-3.0**, and the closest health comparable, Fasten OnPrem, was GPL-3.0 before its repository was archived ([GitHub: immich](https://github.com/immich-app/immich); [nextcloud/server](https://github.com/nextcloud/server); [plausible](https://github.com/plausible/analytics); [mealie](https://github.com/mealie-recipes/mealie); [wger](https://github.com/wger-project/wger); [fasten-onprem](https://github.com/fastenhealth/fasten-onprem)). Precedent strongly supports dropping the CLA. Immich moved from MIT to AGPL in 2024 to prevent a pricing "bait-and-switch", and said a CLA "would bring it back, defeating the point" ([Immich discussion #7023](https://github.com/immich-app/immich/discussions/7023)). It reaffirmed "AGPL without a CLA" after joining FUTO ([Immich joins FUTO](https://immich.app/blog/immich-joins-futo)). Nextcloud's fork from ownCloud was driven partly by contributor dislike of ownCloud's CLA, which existed to enable dual licensing ([FOSS Force](https://fossforce.com/2016/06/owncloud-forked-nextcloud/); [FOSDEM 2018 interview](https://archive.fosdem.org/2018/interviews/frank-karlitschek/)). The other paths carry their own costs. Source-available licenses such as FSL are not OSI open source, and Sentry itself rebranded to "Fair Source" ([Sentry](https://blog.sentry.io/sentry-is-now-fair-source/)). Elastic returned to AGPL in 2024 after its SSPL detour, which it could only do because it held the copyright ([Elastic](https://www.elastic.co/about/open-source)). Cal.com shows that a project controlling its copyright can still go closed: in April 2026 it took its production code private and relaunched the open edition as MIT "Cal.diy" ([Cal.com blog](https://cal.com/blog/cal-com-goes-closed-source-why)).

The consequence for the hosted service follows from the license text. AGPL §13 requires that a **modified** version "prominently offer all users interacting with it remotely… an opportunity to receive the Corresponding Source" ([AGPLv3 via OSI](https://opensource.org/license/agpl-v3)). Self-hosters running unmodified openhealth, even for family over the internet, owe nothing extra. The copyright holder is not bound by its own license, but under DCO the maintainer holds copyright only in their own code. Once contributors' AGPL code is merged, the maintainer's hosted instance is a licensee of that code. In practice, then, the hosted service should run the public source, with its deployment-specific changes published. Any proprietary billing or ops tooling should sit outside the AGPL program as a separate service that talks to it over an API, not as a patch to it. Where exactly that line falls is a **[COUNSEL]** question, and the Bitwarden episode shows how badly it goes when an open-core boundary leaks into the build. In 2024 Bitwarden's GPL desktop client depended on a restrictively licensed SDK, which made the client impossible to build from free code alone, and Bitwarden had to relicense the SDK ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/11/04/bitwarden_gpls_password_manager/)). The upside is that the license, not trust, guarantees self-hosters that the hosted service cannot quietly fork away from them.

AGPL for the mobile app is the one owner decision that the evidence flags as risky, though not fatal. Apple removed a GPL GNU Go port in 2010 rather than change its terms ([FSF](https://www.fsf.org/news/2010-05-app-store-compliance)). VLC for iOS was pulled in 2011 after a single VLC contributor complained, and VLC later relicensed its engine to LGPL partly to fix that ([Wikipedia: VLC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VLC_media_player)). Apple's standard EULA forbids redistribution but carves out "licensing terms governing use of any open-sourced components" ([Apple standard EULA](https://www.apple.com/legal/internet-services/itunes/dev/stdeula/)), and developers can substitute a custom EULA ([App Store Connect help](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/manage-app-information/provide-a-custom-license-agreement)). Copyleft apps ship today: **Signal's iOS and Android apps are AGPL-3.0**, and Bitwarden's and Nextcloud's iOS apps are GPL-3.0 ([Signal-iOS](https://github.com/signalapp/Signal-iOS); [bitwarden/ios](https://github.com/bitwarden/ios); [nextcloud/ios](https://github.com/nextcloud/ios)). The remaining risk is the VLC scenario, where a contributor who holds copyright objects. A DCO certifies where code came from, but it grants no extra permissions. The cheapest mitigation, and an inference for **[COUNSEL]** to confirm, is to add a GPLv3 §7 "additional permission" for app-store distribution to the mobile package before the first external PR lands. Pair it with a custom App Store EULA that defers to the AGPL, and public source and build instructions. Retrofitting that permission later would need consent from every contributor.

Two adjacent obligations come with the license. Dependencies are mostly fine. Apache-2.0 flows into AGPLv3 one way ([Apache](https://www.apache.org/licenses/GPL-compatibility.html)), and MPL-2.0 combines with it unless a file is marked incompatible ([Mozilla MPL FAQ](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/MPL/2.0/FAQ/)). A CI allow-list over `pnpm licenses list`, plus REUSE/SPDX headers and `REUSE.toml` for data files, covers the repo cheaply ([REUSE FAQ](https://reuse.software/faq/)). Bundled datasets are a separate track and keep their own licenses. Open Food Facts is ODbL with attribution and share-alike on adapted databases, which matters if the hosted service enriches and serves that data **[COUNSEL]** ([Open Food Facts terms](https://world.openfoodfacts.org/terms-of-use)). wger's exercise data is CC-BY-SA ([wger](https://github.com/wger-project/wger)), and Creative Commons designates no license compatible with BY-NC-SA 4.0 ([Creative Commons](https://creativecommons.org/share-your-work/licensing-considerations/compatible-licenses/)), so NC data should be excluded entirely. The trademark policy matters as much as the license. Bitwarden's license explicitly grants no trademark rights ([bitwarden/server LICENSE](https://github.com/bitwarden/server/blob/main/LICENSE.txt)), and the trademark is what lets the hosted "openhealth" brand stay distinct from forks.

## Projects that kept extra install paths ended up dropping them

Every comparable project that scaled support settled on **one first-class Compose path** and labelled everything else "community". PostHog dropped Helm in 2023 because its team was "spending an outsized amount of time supporting the 3.5% of users" on Kubernetes ([PostHog](https://posthog.com/blog/sunsetting-helm-support-posthog)). Home Assistant ended support for its Core and Supervised installs, **2.5% and 3.3% of installs**, in 2025.12, because they "generate more issues that are more difficult to solve" ([Home Assistant](https://www.home-assistant.io/blog/2025/05/22/deprecating-core-and-supervised-installation-methods-and-32-bit-systems/)). Immich tags Unraid, TrueNAS and Synology as "[Community]" ([Immich](https://docs.immich.app/install/docker-compose)). Paperless-ngx wraps Compose in an interactive script that "asks a few configuration questions, then creates the required files… and creates your superuser" ([Paperless-ngx setup](https://github.com/paperless-ngx/paperless-ngx/blob/main/docs/setup.md)). openhealth should ship a pinned `compose.yml` with a thin `install.sh` that generates `.env`, and offer no Helm chart in v1. Nextcloud AIO's orchestrator mounts `docker.sock` ([nextcloud/all-in-one](https://github.com/nextcloud/all-in-one)), which is too much privilege and maintenance for a v1 health app. Resource claims should be measured, not borrowed. Plausible needs at least 2 GB with ClickHouse ([Plausible CE](https://github.com/plausible/community-edition)), and Immich needs 6 GB minimum because of its ML stack ([Immich requirements](https://docs.immich.app/install/requirements)). A Bun plus Postgres stack should come in well under both, but nobody has measured that yet.

Configuration should split by when a value is needed. Environment variables, validated at boot by varlock's `.env.schema`, should hold everything required before the database is reachable and every secret: `DATABASE_URL`, the public URL, the auth secret, encryption and backup keys, and SMTP credentials. Runtime product settings such as registration mode, OIDC provider and feature toggles belong in a DB-backed admin UI, with a configuration health page like Mealie's `/admin/site-settings` ([Mealie](https://mealie.io/documentation/getting-started/installation/installation-checklist/)). Immich resolves conflicts between the two cleanly: supplying a config file "will disable the ability to edit other properties from the Immich web UI" ([Immich config file](https://docs.immich.app/install/config-file)). Immich and Plausible both read secrets from `_FILE` variables or `/run/secrets` ([Immich env vars](https://github.com/immich-app/immich/blob/main/docs/docs/install/environment-variables.md); [Plausible CE wiki](https://github.com/plausible/community-edition/wiki/Configuration)), and openhealth should support the same if varlock does not already. Supabase warns that "you should **never** start your self-hosted Supabase using these defaults" ([Supabase](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/self-hosting/docker)), and Cal.diy flags its `secret` placeholder as a production risk ([Cal.diy](https://github.com/calcom/cal.diy)). openhealth's schema should simply refuse to boot on placeholder or too-short secrets. The env reference page should be generated from `.env.schema`, following the table format Immich and Paperless publish.

Upgrades should be `docker compose pull && docker compose up -d`, with forward-only migrations run at startup. Immich ([upgrading](https://docs.immich.app/install/upgrading)), Plausible CE ([compose.yml](https://github.com/plausible/community-edition/blob/HEAD/compose.yml)), Paperless ([administration](https://github.com/paperless-ngx/paperless-ngx/blob/main/docs/administration.md)) and Umami all work this way. Cal.diy's manual migration step is the friction-heavy outlier. Immich limits breaking changes to major versions, publishes `:vN` meta-tags that exclude release candidates, and states plainly that "downgrading… even within the same minor version, is not supported" ([Immich](https://docs.immich.app/install/upgrading)). Plausible recommends pinning to a patch version and does not backport fixes ([Plausible CE wiki: Upgrade](https://github.com/plausible/community-edition/wiki/Upgrade)). openhealth should take these as policy. Pin an exact semver in the shipped Compose file and publish `vX` and `vX.Y` tags. Take a Postgres advisory lock around migrations, print the schema version at boot, and treat rollback as restore-from-backup. That last point means the upgrade path should take a backup first, a lightweight version of Nextcloud AIO's practice of running a backup before every update ([nextcloud/all-in-one](https://github.com/nextcloud/all-in-one)).

## Default credentials and an exposed Postgres are the real threats

**First user becomes admin** is the dominant bootstrap pattern: Immich ([post-install](https://docs.immich.app/install/post-install)), Home Assistant's owner account ([onboarding](https://www.home-assistant.io/getting-started/onboarding/)) and Plausible all use it. The weak pattern still survives elsewhere: Umami ships `admin/umami` ([Umami](https://docs.umami.is/docs/install)) and Mealie ships `changeme@example.com/MyPassword` ([Mealie](https://mealie.io/documentation/getting-started/installation/installation-checklist/)). For a PHI app, first-user-claims-admin has a race, because whoever reaches the URL first owns the instance. None of the sources documents a fix, so this recommendation is an inference. Require a one-time setup token, printed by the install script and to server logs, to claim the owner account. Also support Paperless-style env seeding (`PAPERLESS_ADMIN_USER`), which "will not change an existing user's password" ([Paperless configuration](https://github.com/paperless-ngx/paperless-ngx/blob/main/docs/configuration.md)), for headless installs. After the owner claim, set Better Auth's `disableSignUp: true` ([Better Auth options](https://better-auth.com/docs/reference/options)) and add household members by invite only, matching Plausible's `invite_only` default. Better Auth enables rate limiting in production and limits `/sign-in/email` to 3 requests per 10 seconds. Its defaults keep counters in memory and key on `x-forwarded-for` ([Better Auth rate limit](https://www.better-auth.com/docs/concepts/rate-limit)), so openhealth should store limits in the database and trust only Caddy's forwarding header. Use TOTP or passkeys for the owner ([Better Auth 2FA](https://better-auth.com/docs/plugins/2fa)), and keep password plus TOTP as the fallback because passkeys need an `rpID` that matches a real domain.

The Compose file is where secure defaults matter most. Docker publishes mapped ports on `0.0.0.0` and `[::]` by default and calls this "insecure by default" ([Docker port publishing](https://docs.docker.com/engine/network/port-publishing/)). Its NAT rules divert traffic "before it goes through the ufw firewall settings" ([Docker packet filtering](https://docs.docker.com/engine/network/packet-filtering-firewalls/)), so a user who trusts ufw is still exposed. The threat is active: Border0 counted **over 800,000 exposed Postgres servers**, some breached "multiple times a day", and an extortion bot claimed "All your data is backed up" while keeping only 20 rows per table ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/01/17/extortion_bot_is_autopwning_postgresql/)). The `db` service should therefore have no `ports:` and sit on an internal network. Only Caddy should publish 80 and 443, since it gets certificates automatically "and by default" ([Caddy](https://caddyserver.com/docs/automatic-https)). App containers should run non-root with `cap_drop: [ALL]` and `no-new-privileges`, and all secrets should be generated at install time. Encryption at rest is best handled by host full-disk encryption plus encrypted backups. Column-level encryption adds little when the app server holds the key, though no primary source weighing the two was retrieved.

Telemetry should follow **Home Assistant: "Nothing is sent from your installation unless you explicitly opt in"**. Its analytics come in tiers, are changeable in the UI, and are published only as aggregates ([HA analytics](https://www.home-assistant.io/integrations/analytics/)). Umami's opt-out pixel, which fires in production unless `DISABLE_TELEMETRY` is set ([Umami source](https://github.com/umami-software/umami/blob/master/src/app/api/scripts/telemetry/route.ts)), is the pattern to avoid. Keep local observability separate from phone-home telemetry, as Immich's `IMMICH_TELEMETRY_*` Prometheus variables do ([Immich env vars](https://github.com/immich-app/immich/blob/main/docs/docs/install/environment-variables.md)). Give the update check its own disclosed toggle. A project-run telemetry endpoint also counts as "providing the means" under Recital 18 (see the last section), which is one more reason to keep it opt-in and free of anything health-related.

## Integrations should stay narrow, and push needs a relay that sees only an ID

Comparable projects launch with a small set of adapters configured through env vars: SMTP for email, local accounts plus one OIDC option, and local disk. Plausible's five mail adapters reduce to SMTP relay settings ([Plausible CE wiki](https://github.com/plausible/community-edition/wiki/Configuration)). Actual offers `password`, `openid` and `header` login ([Actual config](https://actualbudget.org/docs/config/)). Supabase simply requires "a production-ready SMTP server" ([Supabase](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/self-hosting/docker)). openhealth's v1 should be SMTP-only email, which covers Resend, SES and Postmark through their SMTP endpoints, and local auth plus generic OIDC. Each capability gets one interface with one or two implementations. Health data sources are where the cost concentrates. Fasten OnPrem "is not able to import data from healthcare providers directly". Its 50,000-institution connectivity stayed in the commercial Fasten Connect, and the self-hosted repository is now archived ([fasten-onprem](https://github.com/fastenhealth/fasten-onprem)). The lesson is to scope self-hosted v1 to file imports and on-device or companion ingestion, such as Health Connect through the phone. Server-side provider OAuth would force every self-hoster to register their own apps, and it should wait.

Mobile apps connect by asking for the server URL at login ([Immich post-install](https://docs.immich.app/install/post-install)), and push is the hard problem. FCM and APNs credentials belong to whoever publishes the app. Nextcloud explains that the keys "can not be shipped with the Nextcloud server as otherwise everyone would have our developer key". Its project-run proxy stores only a public key, device ID and push token, and relays content encrypted with the device's key ([Nextcloud push-v2](https://github.com/nextcloud/notifications/blob/master/docs/push-v2.md)). ntfy's self-hosted servers forward to ntfy.sh only a `poll_request` carrying a message ID, and the device then fetches the content from its own server. On iOS this is unavoidable because instant push is impossible "without a central server" ([ntfy config](https://docs.ntfy.sh/config/)). Home Assistant shows the alternative to avoid: its notification contents "are not encrypted on the Firebase cloud messaging service" ([HA companion](https://companion.home-assistant.io/docs/notifications/notification-details/)). Expo lets anyone with a push token send by default. Enabling its "enhanced push security" requires an access token ([Expo](https://docs.expo.dev/push-notifications/sending-notifications/)), and the project could not hand that token to every self-hoster safely. The recommended design is in two steps. v1 uses only on-device local notifications plus fetch-on-open. Later, a project-run relay, holding the Expo access token and authenticating each instance, forwards only "something changed: ID x", and the app fetches the details over its own TLS connection. That relay is project-operated infrastructure handling metadata about health-app users, so it belongs in the hosted-service privacy analysis. Mobile clients also need publicly trusted HTTPS. Fasten's self-signed root CA, which users had to install on each device, added friction, so the docs should point users to Caddy with Let's Encrypt or to tunnel options. Apple ATS specifics were not verified for this report.

## Backups, export and erasure should be product features, not documentation

The default backup should be a scheduled **`pg_dump -Fc`**. The dump is "internally consistent", does not block normal operation, and "can generally be re-loaded into newer versions of PostgreSQL", whereas PITR is "extremely server-version-specific" and restores only whole clusters ([PostgreSQL: SQL Dump](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/backup-dump.html); [PostgreSQL: PITR](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/continuous-archiving.html)). Immich is the template. It makes daily dumps at 2:00 AM, keeps 14, makes both configurable in the admin UI, and offers restore from the UI and the onboarding screen. It "automatically creates a restore point… and rolls back if restoration fails", and warns that DB dumps do not contain media ([Immich backup](https://docs.immich.app/administration/backup-and-restore/)). openhealth should write dumps next to any attachment directory so that one directory is the thing to back up. Each dump should carry a manifest with app version, schema version, Postgres version and checksums. pgBackRest and WAL-G should be documented as an advanced tier only. Backups should not run while migrations do, because migrations take exclusive locks. For offsite copies, restic or borg encrypt by default, but "losing your password means that your data is irrecoverably lost" ([restic](https://restic.readthedocs.io/en/stable/030_preparing_a_new_repo.html)). Setup should therefore generate the backup passphrase, show it once, ask the owner to store it offline, and never keep it only inside the volume it protects. A `restore-verify` command should load the latest dump into a throwaway Postgres container and report a pass or fail. The ransom bot's fake "backed up" claim is the reason to verify rather than assume.

The legal split between the project and its users depends on who processes the data. GDPR does not apply to "a purely personal or household activity", but it "applies to controllers or processors which provide the means" for that activity ([Recital 18](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-18/)). A household self-hoster is likely exempt. One serving friends or clients likely becomes a controller of Article 9 health data with one-month response duties ([Art. 12](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-12-gdpr/)) **[COUNSEL]**. The project as a pure software supplier is generally neither controller nor processor. Its hosted service, push relay and telemetry endpoint are what bring it into scope. HIPAA largely drops away: data in an app the individual chose, which acts for no covered entity, "is no longer subject to the protections of the HIPAA Rules" ([HHS FAQ 3013](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/3013/does-hipaa-require-a-covered-entity-to-enter-into-a-business-associate-agreement.html)). The FTC HBNR covers PHR vendors whose records "draw information from multiple sources", which describes openhealth. Since the 2024 amendments a "breach" includes unauthorized disclosure, with notice due within **60 days** and penalties up to **$53,088 per violation** ([FTC HBNR](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-ftcs-health-breach-notification-rule-0)). The hosted service should assume it is covered. Whether a non-commercial open-source maintainer counts as a "vendor" is unaddressed **[COUNSEL]**.

| Obligation | Project ships | Self-hoster owns |
|---|---|---|
| Access/portability (Art. 15/20) | Per-user ZIP export: `manifest.json`, versioned JSON, per-type CSV, attachments; optional FHIR R4 Bundle; optional passphrase encryption | Responding within one month if serving non-household users |
| Erasure (Art. 17) | Cascading hard delete across rows, files, caches, AI summaries and old exports; erasure tombstones re-applied after any restore; UI showing when backups age out | Applying retention; rebuilding backups early if required |
| Security | Hardened Compose, no default credentials, opt-in telemetry, security policy and disclosure process | Host hardening, disk encryption, TLS/DNS, backup key custody, breach notification |

The export format follows from Article 20's "structured, commonly used and machine-readable" test ([Art. 20](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-20-gdpr/)). Versioned JSON gives round-trip fidelity and CSV serves spreadsheet users. FHIR R4, typed with `@medplum/fhirtypes` ([Medplum](https://www.medplum.com/docs/fhir-datastore/working-with-fhir)), should be labelled "interoperability, not lossless backup": supplements and workouts map poorly, and LOINC codes should never be invented. For backups, the UK ICO accepts holding erased data "beyond use" until scheduled rotation replaces it ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/individual-rights/right-to-erasure/)). The 14-dump retention default therefore doubles as a compliance mechanism. That position comes from UK guidance and was not checked against EDPB guidance **[COUNSEL]**. One gap remains: whether Health Connect and HealthKit terms restrict re-exporting data synced through them was not checked.

## Conclusion

Most of these decisions answer one question: who carries the risk when an operator gets it wrong. Self-hosted health data fails quietly. A published Postgres port, a backup passphrase stored inside the volume it protects, an unclaimed admin URL, or a push payload containing a lab value each turns a privacy product into a breach without anyone noticing. Precedent supports moving each of those risks out of the docs and into code: refuse to boot on bad secrets, never publish the DB port, gate the owner claim with a token, check restores automatically, and carry only an ID in push. The licensing evidence points the same way from the other side. AGPL plus DCO without a CLA removes the maintainer's ability to relicense or quietly diverge, and that is exactly what makes the free self-hosted edition credible next to a paid hosted one.

The less obvious finding concerns the hosted service. Planning it changes the project's obligations more than any self-hosting decision does. It is the point where GDPR Recital 18 and the FTC HBNR reach the maintainer, where AGPL §13 applies to the maintainer's own deployment, and where the push relay and telemetry endpoint become regulated infrastructure. Four questions should go to counsel before the hosted service or the first external mobile contribution, because none of them can be cheaply fixed afterwards. Does a §7 app-store permission need to be in the mobile package before contributors arrive? Where may proprietary hosted tooling sit relative to the AGPL program? Is the project covered by the HBNR? Is a relay that handles only IDs outside it?
