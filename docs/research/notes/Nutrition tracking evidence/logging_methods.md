# Diet Self-Monitoring Methods: Evidence for OpenValens Logging Features

Scope: manual logging, barcode, AI photo recognition, voice/natural-language logging, quick-add, recipes. Adults 18+, wellness context. Current as of October 2026. Evidence-type labels used: [SR] systematic review, [RCT], [Obs] observational/secondary analysis, [Val] validation study, [Lab] controlled lab/benchmark, [Pilot], [Preprint] not peer reviewed, [Abstract] conference abstract only, [Reg] regulatory/standards text.

Research-process note: the web-search budget for this session ran out partway through, so the privacy (EXIF/faces), robots.txt/scraping law, barcode accuracy and favourites/quick-add questions are thin. They are listed under Gaps and should not be read as "no evidence exists".

## 1. Dietary self-monitoring effects on weight outcomes and adherence decay

### Takeaway

How often people log is consistently linked to more weight loss, but nearly all of that evidence is correlational. Logging also drops off over weeks to months. Simplified logging can keep adherence far higher with similar short-term weight loss, so cutting burden matters more than detail.

### Cited Findings

- [SR] Burke, Wang & Sevick 2011 (J Am Diet Assoc; 22 studies, 1993–2009) found "a significant association between self-monitoring and weight loss" for diet, exercise and self-weighing. The authors rated the evidence quality weak because of methodological limits. Most studies used paper diaries, only 5 used technology (Internet, PDAs), and samples were "predominantly white and women". DOI 10.1016/j.jada.2010.10.008 — [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE%3A%22Self-monitoring%20in%20weight%20loss%3A%20a%20systematic%20review%20of%20the%20literature%22&resultType=core&format=json)
- [Obs, secondary analysis of a 24-week online behavioural program] Harvey, Krukowski, Priest & West 2019, "Log Often, Lose More" (Obesity, DOI 10.1002/oby.22382). People who lost at least 5% or at least 10% logged in to the food journal more times per day (1.6 vs 2.4 for <5% vs ≥5%; 1.7 vs 2.7 for <10% vs ≥10%; P<0.001). The authors concluded that "the time needed to be successful diminish[ed] during the intervention" — [Europe PMC abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE%3A%22Log%20Often%2C%20Lose%20More%22&resultType=core&format=json); [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/oby.22382)
  - Secondary summaries say self-monitoring took about 15–23 minutes/day and that people who lost ≥10% recorded on at least 20 days/month. I did not verify these figures against the abstract text. Check them in the full paper before quoting — [search summary of Wiley page](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/oby.22382)
- [RCT, n=100, 12 weeks, MyFitnessPal] Patel, Hopkins, Brooks & Bennett 2019 (JMIR mHealth uHealth, DOI 10.2196/12209). Weight loss by arm: Sequential (weight first, diet from week 5) −2.7 kg, Simultaneous (weight + diet) −2.8 kg, App-Only (diet only, no behavioural support) −2.4 kg. Differences were not significant, and about one-third of participants reached ≥5% loss. Median diet tracking was 5.3 days/week in Simultaneous vs 1.9 days/week in Sequential; Sequential tracked on 27% of days vs 77% for Simultaneous once diet tracking began. Logging more often was associated with losing more weight — [PMC6416539](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6416539/)
- [SR] Patel, Wakayama & Bennett 2021 (Obesity; 39 trials, 2009–2019; this is probably the "Patel 2019" the brief meant). Digital self-monitoring was associated with greater weight loss across trials. Diet was tracked in 81% of 67 digital interventions. A search summary reported that in the first 3 months, 3/8 interventions (38%) reached ≥4 tracking days/week. The same summary gave figures for longer interventions (73% at 6–11 months, 0/1 at ≥12 months) that look odd and cannot be confirmed from the abstract. Verify before using — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/oby.23088); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33624440/)
- [RCT] Patel et al. 2022 (JMIR Formative Research, DOI 10.2196/42191; racial and ethnic minority adults). Weight loss was the same for detailed and simplified dietary tracking (−3.4 kg vs −3.3 kg). Median days tracked were 49% for detailed vs 97% for simplified — [Europe PMC](<https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE%3A%22Is%20Burden%20Always%20Bad%22%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Comparing%20Self-Monitoring%20Strategies%20for%20Weight%20Loss%22%20OR%20(TITLE%3A%22self-monitoring%22%20AND%20TITLE%3A%22simplified%22%20AND%20diet)%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Adherence%20to%20mobile-app-based%20dietary%20self-monitoring%22&resultType=core&format=json>)
- [Pilot RCT] Nezami et al. 2022 (Obesity, DOI 10.1002/oby.23377). Six-month weight loss was 5.7% with standard calorie tracking and 4.0% with simplified tracking. Confidence intervals overlapped, and there were "no differences in dietary tracking adherence" — [Europe PMC, same query](<https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE%3A%22Is%20Burden%20Always%20Bad%22%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Comparing%20Self-Monitoring%20Strategies%20for%20Weight%20Loss%22%20OR%20(TITLE%3A%22self-monitoring%22%20AND%20TITLE%3A%22simplified%22%20AND%20diet)%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Adherence%20to%20mobile-app-based%20dietary%20self-monitoring%22&resultType=core&format=json>)
- [Obs] Payne et al. 2022 (Obesity Science & Practice, DOI 10.1002/osp4.566; N=90, 8 weeks). Mean weight change was −1.5 ± 2.1 kg, and "consistent and frequent app-based dietary self-monitoring were associated with short-term weight loss" — [Europe PMC, same query](<https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE%3A%22Is%20Burden%20Always%20Bad%22%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Comparing%20Self-Monitoring%20Strategies%20for%20Weight%20Loss%22%20OR%20(TITLE%3A%22self-monitoring%22%20AND%20TITLE%3A%22simplified%22%20AND%20diet)%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Adherence%20to%20mobile-app-based%20dietary%20self-monitoring%22&resultType=core&format=json>)
- Other relevant work, found but not read: a review titled "Is Burden Always Bad? Emerging Low-Burden Approaches to Mobile Dietary Self-monitoring" (Springer, J Technol Behav Sci) — [link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41347-021-00203-9). A study on minimum self-monitoring thresholds for weight-loss maintenance — [PMC10972539](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10972539/). A study of within-week and within-year logging patterns — [PMC11966970](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11966970/)

### Inferences

- Almost all of the frequency-to-outcome evidence is correlational, and the populations are mostly women with overweight or obesity in structured weight-loss programs. It may not carry over to a general wellness audience. Product copy should not claim that logging _causes_ weight loss.
- Simplified tracking roughly doubled adherence in one RCT with no loss in short-term weight outcome. This supports making a low-detail mode a first-class option (quick-add kcal, "tracked today: yes/no", single-number entries), alongside full nutrient logging.
- Harvey found that the time needed to log well fell over the intervention. That suggests learned efficiency, which recents, favourites and meal copy are meant to speed up.
- For the UI, "days logged per week" is a reasonable adherence signal. Show it neutrally and do not shame users for gaps.

### Gaps

- I could not verify Harvey's minutes/day figures or the Patel 2021 decline-by-duration figures against the full text.
- I found no adequately powered RCT isolating the effect of logging _modality_ (photo vs voice vs manual) on weight outcomes.

## 2. Accuracy of manual app logging vs weighed records / doubly labelled water; portion-size error

### Takeaway

Under lab conditions, manual logging in a large app (MyFitnessPal) can come within about 7% of weighed energy. Errors in individual meals and nutrients are larger, and depend heavily on the database and the user. Self-reported intake in free-living conditions typically falls 17–30% short of doubly labelled water (DLW).

### Cited Findings

- [Lab RCT crossover, n=43] Lozano et al. 2024 (AJCN, DOI 10.1016/j.ajcnut.2024.05.023). MyFitnessPal energy was overestimated by 7.0% and was statistically equivalent to weighed food (P=0.04). The PortionSize app underestimated energy by 13.3% and portion size by 14.0%, and was not equivalent — [Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI%3A10.1016%2Fj.ajcnut.2024.05.024%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Validity%20of%20the%20PortionSize%20application%20compared%20with%20that%20of%20MyFitnessPal%22%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Customized%20multimodal%20Diabot-GPT-4o%22%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Limited%20validity%20of%20an%20AI-powered%20app%20for%20dietary%20assessment%22&resultType=core&format=json); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38825184/)
- [Val] MyFitnessPal database vs the Belgian Nubel database (JMIR 2020, DOI 10.2196/18237): accurate for energy, macronutrients, sugar and fibre, but not for cholesterol and sodium. A search summary also reported percent errors vs weighed measures of −18.8% for fat, +5.8% for protein and +11.3% for carbohydrate. Those figures were not verified against the full text — [JMIR](https://doi.org/10.2196/18237)
- [Lab, researcher-entered standard menus] Li et al. 2024 (Nutrients; 16 manual-logging apps). Mean energy error varied with diet type: Western diet +1040 kJ, Asian diet −1520 kJ, recommended (Australian guideline) diet −944 kJ — [PMC11314244](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11314244/)
- [Val vs DLW] Remote Food Photography Method in adolescents (Höchsmann et al., AJCN 2026, DOI 10.1016/j.ajcnut.2026.101452). Free-living intake was underestimated by a mean (SD) of −887.9 (554.8) kcal/d in study 1 and −782.8 (947.0) kcal/d in study 2. A summary described this as roughly 25–30%; the percentage is a summary figure, not a quoted one — [Europe PMC](<https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=(%22portion%20size%22%20AND%20estimation%20AND%20error%20AND%20(app%20OR%20smartphone)%20AND%20(%22doubly%20labeled%20water%22%20OR%20%22doubly%20labelled%20water%22))&resultType=core&format=json&pageSize=8>)
- [Val] PortionSize app in children (Saha et al., Pediatric Obesity 2026): mean percent error 9.1% in a controlled setting and 16.5% in free-living conditions, with systematic underestimation in free-living use. The population is children, so this is outside OpenValens's scope; it is cited only for the controlled vs free-living gap — [same Europe PMC query](<https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=(%22portion%20size%22%20AND%20estimation%20AND%20error%20AND%20(app%20OR%20smartphone)%20AND%20(%22doubly%20labeled%20water%22%20OR%20%22doubly%20labelled%20water%22))&resultType=core&format=json&pageSize=8>)
- [Obs, DLW] Professional male soccer players (Eur J Sport Sci 2026): photo-method intake was 10.95 MJ/d against DLW energy expenditure of 13.25 MJ/d, about 17% lower. This is a niche population — [same query](<https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=(%22portion%20size%22%20AND%20estimation%20AND%20error%20AND%20(app%20OR%20smartphone)%20AND%20(%22doubly%20labeled%20water%22%20OR%20%22doubly%20labelled%20water%22))&resultType=core&format=json&pageSize=8>)
- [Background] DLW studies indicate that self-report generally estimates energy intake poorly, with weighed multi-day records as a possible exception — [nutritionalassessment.org](https://nutritionalassessment.org/validity/index.html)

### Inferences

- Lab equivalence (±7%) is the best case: trained or attentive users with known portions. Free-living underreporting of 15–30% is the realistic floor for any self-report method, including manual logging. OpenValens should present logged intake as an "estimate" and avoid kcal precision beyond about 10 kcal.
- Database quality is a separate error source from portion error. Crowd-sourced entries are a known risk, and sodium and cholesterol are weak. Prefer verified sources (USDA FDC, national databases) and show the data source for each food (this fits the repo's provenance rules).
- An optional kitchen-scale/grams input should be easy to reach, because weighed records are the most accurate self-report approach.

### Gaps

- I found no study of MyFitnessPal or other consumer manual app logging against DLW in free-living adults.
- I found no peer-reviewed accuracy data for barcode scanning (label errors, database mismatch). Search budget was exhausted.

## 3. AI image-based dietary assessment: accuracy and failure modes

### Takeaway

Current multimodal LLMs recognise foods well (often around 90%) and can get within about 10–25% of energy on simple, standardised plates. Performance degrades on mixed dishes, high-fat and keto meals, and in free-living use. A consistent failure mode is mis-estimating invisible fat. Free-living validation against DLW showed large underestimation (about 25%) and no individual-level reliability. Added context such as a text description, food names or meal time measurably improves estimates, which supports a human-in-the-loop design.

### Cited Findings

**Peer-reviewed, consumer apps**

- [Lab] Li et al. 2024 (Nutrients; 7 AI photo apps; standard menus photographed by researchers). Food component identification: MyFitnessPal 97%, Fastic 92%, HealthifyMe 90%, Foodvisor 87%. Mean energy difference on single items ranged from −3% (MyFitnessPal) and +8% (HealthifyMe) to −47% (Foodvisor) and +44% (Fastic) — [PMC11314244](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11314244/)
- [Val vs DLW, free-living] Serra et al. 2026 (npj Digital Medicine, DOI 10.1038/s41746-026-02536-2; n=20 females with obesity; SNAQ AI app). Energy was underestimated by 25% (bias −817 kcal/day; limits of agreement −3707 to +2073 kcal/day), and within-subject reliability was ICC = 0.00. Title: "Limited validity of an AI-powered app…" — [Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI%3A10.1016%2Fj.ajcnut.2024.05.024%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Validity%20of%20the%20PortionSize%20application%20compared%20with%20that%20of%20MyFitnessPal%22%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Customized%20multimodal%20Diabot-GPT-4o%22%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Limited%20validity%20of%20an%20AI-powered%20app%20for%20dietary%20assessment%22&resultType=core&format=json)
- [Abstract, not peer reviewed] Hengist & Charles (NIDDK/NIH Clinical Center), NUTRITION 2026 (ASN), July 2026. They tested MyFitnessPal, Lose It!, Cal AI and Appediet on 102 metabolic-kitchen meals weighed to 0.1 g, plus 200+ more meals. Every app underestimated energy by about 250–345 kcal/meal and fat by about 30 g/meal. Carbohydrate was more consistent, and keto (high-fat) meals were harder — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260726015237.htm); [Medscape](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/photo-based-meal-apps-underestimate-calories-and-fat-2026a1000qo9)

**Peer-reviewed, general multimodal LLMs**

- [Lab] Isobe et al. 2026 (Nutrients; 15 standardised hospital meals under controlled photography; 10 AI systems incl. ChatGPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro, Claude 3.5 Sonnet and 7 dietary apps, vs 10 registered dietitians):
  - Energy: ChatGPT-4o r=0.89, MAE 38.5 kcal, 73.3% of meals within ±10%. Gemini 1.5 Pro r=0.85, MAE 42.1 kcal, 60.0% within ±10%. Dietitians r=0.92, MAE 22.4 kcal, 86.7% within ±10%.
  - Every AI model overestimated lipids (mean bias > +20%, p<0.01), and fewer than 20% of meals were within ±10% for fat. Protein was overestimated by 10–21%.
  - The authors recommend a human-in-the-loop design and capturing cooking metadata.
  - [PMC13029357](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13029357/)
  - Note: fat is *over*estimated here (hospital meals) but *under*estimated in the NIH abstract (metabolic-kitchen and keto meals). The direction of fat error varies with meal type. Fat error being large is consistent across both.
- [Val vs weighed records] Chen et al. 2025 (AJCN, DOI 10.1016/j.ajcnut.2025.10.013; 714 images from 57 young-adult dietetic trainees in Taiwan over 171 days). A customised GPT-4o pipeline ("Diabot") estimated portions within ±10–15% (CV 13%) and energy within ±10–20% (CV 14%), with Spearman ρ 0.66–0.86 against weighed records. From images alone it recognised 74% of items vs 59% for non-customised GPT-4o. The participants were trained in dietetics, so this is not a general population — [Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI%3A10.1016%2Fj.ajcnut.2024.05.024%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Validity%20of%20the%20PortionSize%20application%20compared%20with%20that%20of%20MyFitnessPal%22%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Customized%20multimodal%20Diabot-GPT-4o%22%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Limited%20validity%20of%20an%20AI-powered%20app%20for%20dietary%20assessment%22&resultType=core&format=json); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41138916/)
- [Pilot, 2025, ScienceDirect journal S0889-1575, likely J Food Compos Anal] "Image-based nutritional assessment: ChatGPT-4o on simple and complex meals". Initial predictions erred by up to 54.4% for energy and 76.5% for fat, especially on complex meals, and accuracy improved significantly when users added contextual input. Taken from the search abstract; I could not fetch the full text (403) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S088915752501659X)
- Another candidate source, not read: Reasoning-Driven Food Energy Estimation via MLLMs — [PMC11990770](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11990770/)

**Preprints (arXiv, not peer reviewed)**

- [Preprint] Coburn et al., July 2025, arXiv:2507.07048. ACETADA dataset of 806 controlled-feeding images with dietitian-verified labels. Baseline energy MAPE was about 23–24%: GPT-4.1 23.29%, Claude 3.7 Sonnet 23.88%, GPT-4o 24.48%. Adding metadata (GPS-derived location, timestamp, food item names) cut calorie MAE by about 76 kcal on average, and portion error by about 53 g. Reasoning prompts such as an expert persona or chain-of-thought helped further — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.07048v1)
- [Preprint] Liao & Li, July 2026, arXiv:2607.16514. On 100 multi-food Nutrition5k dishes, per-food portion MAE was: Gemini-3.5-Flash 24.1 g (34.7%), Gemini-3.1-Pro 25.8 g (36.6%), GPT-5.5 26.7 g (38.7%), Claude-Fable-5 35.7 g (51.3%), Claude-Opus-4.8 36.2 g (52.9%). The authors argue that MLLMs "recognize a wide range of foods zero-shot" but struggle with portion size. Their geometry head (frozen DINOv2) cut portion error by 33–41%. The per-model numbers come from a search-result summary, while the abstract was fetched directly — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.16514)

**Whether users correct estimates**

- Isobe et al. and the ChatGPT-4o complex-meal pilot both find that adding human context or correction improves accuracy — [PMC13029357](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13029357/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S088915752501659X)
- The NIH abstract cautions that people using photo apps _without_ adjusting portions should treat the results with scepticism — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260726015237.htm)

### Inferences

- Present AI output as an editable draft, not a measurement:
  - show recognised items, assumed portions (grams or household units) and assumed cooking method or added fat;
  - require or prompt confirmation before saving;
  - store the raw AI estimate, the user-edited values and the model/version as provenance.
- Communicate uncertainty with ranges, e.g. "±20–25% typical; mixed dishes and oily foods can be much further off", rather than single precise numbers. Flag fat and oil explicitly, e.g. "Was this cooked in oil or butter?".
- Asking for context (a text description, "cooked in X tbsp oil", portion hints) is cheap and the evidence shows it helps. Make it a one-tap follow-up.
- Metadata helped in the Coburn preprint, but GPS is privacy-sensitive (see section 7). Meal time is a safer piece of context to send than location.
- Benchmarks on a single public dataset change fast and are mostly preprints. Avoid hard-coding a model choice on the strength of one leaderboard, and build a small in-house evaluation set instead.
- Free-living individual-level validity is poor (ICC 0.00 in Serra). AI photo logging is fine for awareness and trends, but should not be presented as clinically accurate for individual intake.

### Gaps

- I found no peer-reviewed study measuring how often real users edit AI estimates, or whether their edits improve accuracy in free-living use. Claims on vendor blogs that "AI learns from edits" are unsupported.
- I found no peer-reviewed evaluation of Claude 4.x or 5.x models on DLW-validated free-living intake. The model comparisons are lab or benchmark preprints only.
- The NIH 2026 work is an abstract only. Watch for the journal publication.

## 4. Voice / natural-language meal logging

### Takeaway

Evidence is thin and pilot-scale, but voice logging improved engagement over text in one small RCT. Accuracy of nutrient estimates from spoken or typed descriptions is largely unvalidated in peer-reviewed literature. Whisper-class ASR is technically strong. Accent robustness and parsing to a food database are the realistic bottlenecks.

### Cited Findings

- [Pilot RCT, n=18, 28 days] Chikwetu, Daily, Mortazavi & Dunn 2023 (JMIR Formative Research). The base2Diet iOS app transcribed speech with Apple's on-device speech framework and resolved nutrients through the Nutritionix natural-language API. Compared with text logging, the voice arm had:
  - 1.7× more logging events (30 vs 18; p=.03);
  - 1.5× more active days (19 vs 13; p=.04);
  - dropout of 11% vs 56%;
  - 83% of users agreeing it made monitoring easier.
  - Word error rate was not reported, and transcription struggled with foreign accents.
  - [PMC10230351](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10230351/)
- [Abstract] Coco, a conversational calorie counter for iOS (Current Developments in Nutrition, 2023 conference abstract), mapped natural-language meal descriptions to the USDA database. Perceived accuracy of nutrition facts was rated 1.86 ± 0.53 on a scale where 1 is best — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2475299123151687)
- [Registered study] "Food Talk" (Tufts/MIT): an app for self-monitoring with natural spoken language that estimates daily energy and nutrients — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03869658](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03869658)
- [Preprint] arXiv:2509.13268 studied LLM energy and macronutrient estimation from text-only 24-hour recall data using parameter-efficient fine-tuning with a 10-shot prompt. Found in search but not read in detail — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.13268)
- [Preprint, Whisper] Radford et al. 2022, "Robust Speech Recognition via Large-Scale Weak Supervision" (arXiv:2212.04356). Trained on 680,000 hours of multilingual, multitask supervision, it is zero-shot competitive with fully supervised systems and "approach[es]" human accuracy and robustness. My recollection is that it later appeared at ICML 2023, but I did not verify that in this session — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.04356)
- Vendor and blog claims (e.g. "85–90% recognition for common foods by voice") were found but are unsourced marketing and were excluded as evidence — e.g. [healthyoneapp blog](https://www.healthyoneapp.com/blog/best-voice-food-tracker-app-2026)

### Inferences

- Voice and natural-language entry are promising for reducing burden. Engagement is the best-supported benefit, from n=18. Accuracy is bounded by the same portion problem as manual logging, plus parsing errors.
- Architecture: speech-to-text (on-device or self-hosted Whisper/faster-whisper is feasible for an OSS project and avoids sending audio to third parties), then an LLM or rule parser into structured items and quantities, then a lookup in a verified database. Always show the parsed items for confirmation before saving.
- Typed natural language ("2 eggs and toast with butter") gives most of the voice benefit without ASR risk, and is the cheapest to build first.

### Gaps

- I found no peer-reviewed validation of NL or voice-derived nutrient estimates against weighed records or DLW in adults.
- I found no published faster-whisper benchmarks on food vocabulary or accented speech in this domain, and I did not verify faster-whisper latency or memory on mobile.

## 5. Recipe import (schema.org Recipe JSON-LD): reliability and legal considerations

### Takeaway

schema.org/Recipe gives a standard structure, but `recipeIngredient` is usually free text, so ingredient-to-food matching and quantity parsing are still needed. In the US, ingredient lists are not copyrightable but expressive instructions may be. Storing parsed ingredients plus a source URL, without copying full instruction prose, is the lower-risk pattern.

### Cited Findings

- [Reg/Standard] On schema.org/Recipe, `recipeIngredient` accepts free text (e.g. "3 or 4 ripe bananas, smashed") or structured PropertyValue with quantity and unit. `recipeInstructions` can be text, HowToStep or HowToSection. `recipeYield` can be a QuantitativeValue or text, and `nutrition` is a NutritionInformation object. Publisher-provided nutrition is optional — [schema.org/Recipe](https://schema.org/Recipe)
- [Reg] US Copyright Office Circular 33: "Recipes and lists of ingredients are not protected by copyright". Simple instructions lack the creative expression copyright requires, but material containing "original literary expression" may be protected — [copyright.gov Circular 33](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ33.pdf)

### Inferences

- Import pipeline:
  1. Fetch the page and extract JSON-LD `@type: Recipe`.
  2. Parse ingredient strings into quantity, unit and food, and map each to the database with user confirmation.
  3. Compute nutrition per serving from `recipeYield`.
  4. Store the source URL as provenance.
- Treat publisher `nutrition` values as a cross-check only.
- To lower legal risk: import on the user's request (not bulk crawling), keep ingredients and numbers, link back to the source for instructions or store them only privately for that user, honour robots.txt and rate limits, and don't redistribute imported recipes publicly.
- Manual recipe building (combining foods into a reusable dish) is the core feature. URL import is a convenience layered on top.

### Gaps

- I found no empirical data on how many recipe sites expose valid JSON-LD, or on ingredient-parser accuracy.
- I did not research robots.txt legal status, site terms-of-service enforceability, or EU and other copyright and database rights (e.g. the EU sui generis database right). Search budget was exhausted. A legal review is advisable for any non-US hosting.

## 6. Burden-reduction features (favourites, recents, meal copy, quick-add)

### Takeaway

I found no direct trials isolating favourites, recents, meal copy or quick-add. Indirect evidence supports them: simplified tracking roughly doubled adherence with equivalent weight loss, voice cut dropout, and the time needed to log fell with practice.

### Cited Findings

- Simplified vs detailed tracking: 97% vs 49% of days tracked, with equivalent weight loss (Patel et al. 2022) — [Europe PMC](<https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE%3A%22Is%20Burden%20Always%20Bad%22%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Comparing%20Self-Monitoring%20Strategies%20for%20Weight%20Loss%22%20OR%20(TITLE%3A%22self-monitoring%22%20AND%20TITLE%3A%22simplified%22%20AND%20diet)%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Adherence%20to%20mobile-app-based%20dietary%20self-monitoring%22&resultType=core&format=json>)
- Simplified vs standard calorie tracking: no difference in adherence; 4.0% vs 5.7% weight loss with overlapping confidence intervals (Nezami et al. 2022) — [same source](<https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE%3A%22Is%20Burden%20Always%20Bad%22%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Comparing%20Self-Monitoring%20Strategies%20for%20Weight%20Loss%22%20OR%20(TITLE%3A%22self-monitoring%22%20AND%20TITLE%3A%22simplified%22%20AND%20diet)%20OR%20TITLE%3A%22Adherence%20to%20mobile-app-based%20dietary%20self-monitoring%22&resultType=core&format=json>)
- Time needed for successful self-monitoring decreased over the intervention (Harvey 2019) — [Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE%3A%22Log%20Often%2C%20Lose%20More%22&resultType=core&format=json)
- Voice vs text: dropout 11% vs 56% (Chikwetu 2023, n=18) — [PMC10230351](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10230351/)
- A Europe PMC search on favourites, recent foods, quick-add and copy-meal in app self-monitoring returned no studies that evaluated those features directly — [Europe PMC query](<https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=(food%20OR%20diet)%20AND%20(favorites%20OR%20%22recent%20foods%22%20OR%20%22quick%20add%22%20OR%20%22copy%20meal%22)%20AND%20(self-monitoring%20OR%20logging)%20AND%20app&resultType=core&format=json&pageSize=10>)

### Inferences

- Build recents, favourites, saved meals and copy-yesterday as table stakes. They are low cost and consistent with the burden evidence, but say they are "evidence-consistent", not "evidence-proven".
- Quick-add kcal (and optional macros) supports the simplified-tracking pattern. Label quick-add entries as lower-detail so nutrient summaries can report completeness, e.g. "X% of kcal has full nutrient data".
- Instrument time-to-log and days-logged per method (privately, aggregate-only) so OpenValens can produce its own evidence.

### Gaps

- No RCT or usability study isolating favourites, recents, copy or quick-add was found. Search budget was exhausted, and there may be HCI literature (CHI, CSCW) that I did not search.

## 7. Privacy of food photos and third-party AI processing

### Takeaway

I could not gather evidence on this in this session. Food photos can carry EXIF GPS, device data and timestamps, and may include faces, other people, documents or home interiors. Sending them to third-party AI is a privacy decision. One preprint shows GPS and time metadata improve AI estimates, which creates a direct accuracy-vs-privacy tension.

### Cited Findings

- [Preprint] Location (GPS-derived) and timestamp metadata reduced AI calorie MAE by about 76 kcal on average, and by up to 246 kcal for one open model (Coburn et al. 2025). Location data has accuracy value, which creates an incentive to collect it — [arXiv:2507.07048](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.07048v1)
- [Pilot] base2Diet used Apple's on-device speech framework, an example of keeping raw audio local while sending only text to a nutrient API (Nutritionix) — [PMC10230351](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10230351/)

### Inferences

These are engineering recommendations, not evidence-based findings:

- Strip EXIF (GPS, device serial, maker notes) on the client before upload.
- Optionally downscale or crop images to the plate.
- Never send location to an AI provider by default. If context is useful, send a coarse meal type and time instead.
- Make third-party AI processing opt-in and consent-gated per provider, and show which provider processes the image.
- Offer a local or self-hosted model path where feasible.
- Don't retain images by default after estimation, or let the user choose.
- Record provider and model version as provenance.
- Check the provider's data-retention and training-use terms. I did not verify current OpenAI, Google or Anthropic API retention terms in this session.

### Gaps

- I found no empirical sources on EXIF prevalence in food photos, on faces or bystanders in meal images, or on user attitudes to AI processing of food photos. Search budget was exhausted. This needs a follow-up search, e.g. "EXIF GPS smartphone photo privacy study", "dietary image bystander privacy", and GDPR status of food photos as health data.
- I did not research the regulatory classification of food images (GDPR Article 9 health data or not) or the HIPAA applicability of a wellness app.
