# Evidence behind popular "longevity" recommendations and routine extra testing (for openhealth)

Scope: healthy/asymptomatic adults 18+, wellness context, no diagnosis. Excludes jurisdictional screening schedules and app regulatory status (owned by other researchers). Current as of 2 Oct 2026. Evidence type and population are stated per finding; where only a secondary source (news/summary) was reached, that is flagged.

---

## 1. Supplements in healthy adults (vitamin D, omega-3, creatine, magnesium, multivitamins) and interactions

### Takeaway
Large primary-prevention RCTs (VITAL, D-Health, ASCEND, STRENGTH, COSMOS) show no meaningful reduction in hard outcomes (CVD, cancer, all-cause mortality) from vitamin D, fish-oil omega-3, or multivitamins in generally healthy/older adults; USPSTF 2022 rates multivitamins/most single nutrients "insufficient evidence" and recommends against beta-carotene and vitamin E. Omega-3 carries a dose-related atrial fibrillation signal. Creatine and magnesium have small, mostly surrogate/symptom-level benefits with low-to-moderate certainty. None should be framed as a longevity intervention; at most "optional, low-certainty, check interactions".

### Cited Findings

**Vitamin D**
- VITAL (NEJM 2019; ~25,000 US adults, men >=50, women >=55, 2000 IU/day D3, median 5.3 y): total invasive cancer HR 0.96 (95% CI 0.88–1.06); major CVD events HR 0.97 (0.85–1.12); MI HR 0.96, stroke HR 0.95, CVD mortality HR 1.11 (all non-significant). A cancer-mortality signal appeared only in analyses excluding early follow-up (secondary/post hoc). — [Manson, principal VITAL results + meta-analyses, J Steroid Biochem Mol Biol](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960076019305424); [VITAL findings page](https://www.vitalstudy.org/findings.html); [TCTMD summary](https://www.tctmd.com/news/vital-no-benefits-vitamin-d-and-omega-3s-reducing-major-cv-events-cancer)
- D-Health (Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol, Jan 2022; N=21,315 Australians aged 60–84, 60,000 IU D3 monthly vs placebo, 5 y; all-cause mortality primary outcome): did not reduce all-cause mortality in unscreened older people; no effect on CVD mortality. — [Neale et al., Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landia/article/PIIS2213-8587(21)00345-4/abstract)
  - Cancer mortality: point estimates were numerically *higher* in the vitamin D group with wide CIs crossing 1, and higher still when excluding the first 2 years. — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2213858721003454). Note: one aggregator search summary claimed "a reduction in cancer mortality was observed"; this contradicts the abstract and should be disregarded.
- Endocrine Society 2024 Clinical Practice Guideline (JCEM, presented 3 June 2024): suggests **against routine 25(OH)D testing** in all populations studied — including adults with dark complexion, obesity, and in pregnancy — because outcome-specific benefits based on levels have not been identified; panel could not define 25(OH)D thresholds for adequacy or disease-prevention targets. Healthy adults <75 are advised to get the recommended daily allowance (not high-dose empiric supplementation). — [Endocrine Society guideline page](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/vitamin-d-for-prevention-of-disease); [Endocrine Society press release](https://www.endocrine.org/news-and-advocacy/news-room/2024/endocrine-society-recommends-healthy-adults-take-the-recommended-daily-allowance-of-vitamin-d); [Medscape: "Don't Screen"](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/dont-screen-vitamin-d-new-endo-society-guideline-2024a1000aez); [critical appraisal, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13164739/)

**Omega-3 (fish oil)**
- VITAL omega-3 arm (1 g/day, ~25,000 adults, median 5.3 y): primary CVD composite 386 vs 419 events, HR 0.92 (0.80–1.06; P=0.24). A 28% reduction in total MI was a secondary finding (largest in low fish intake and African American participants) — hypothesis-generating only. — [2 Minute Medicine summary](https://www.2minutemedicine.com/the-vital-trial-omega-3-supplementation-does-not-reduce-risk-of-cardiovascular-events/); [VITAL findings](https://www.vitalstudy.org/findings.html)
- ASCEND (NEJM 2018; ~15,000 adults with diabetes, no CVD, 1 g/day): serious vascular events 8.9% vs 9.2% (p=0.55) — no benefit. — [ACC trial summary](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Clinical-Trials/2018/08/25/02/19/ASCEND-Omega-3); [NEJM full text PDF mirror](https://natap.org/2018/HIV/nejmoa1804989.pdf)
- STRENGTH (JAMA 2020; high-CV-risk statin-treated patients, 4 g/day omega-3 carboxylic acid vs corn oil): primary MACE 12.0% vs 12.2%, HR 0.99 (0.90–1.09; P=0.84); stopped early for futility; higher AF in treatment arm. — [JAMA](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2778119); [ACC summary](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Clinical-Trials/2020/11/11/21/29/STRENGTH)
- REDUCE-IT (icosapent ethyl 4 g/day, a prescription EPA drug, in high-risk patients with elevated triglycerides) reported benefit, but its mineral-oil comparator raised LDL-C (+10.9% median at 12 months vs −1.2% with IPE), ApoB and hsCRP, so the apparent benefit may be partly placebo harm; this cannot be resolved within REDUCE-IT's design. Population and product are not relevant to OTC fish oil in healthy adults. — [Circulation biomarker substudy](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.122.059410); [Circulation: "Summoning STRENGTH to question the placebo"](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.121.054539); [TCTMD](https://www.tctmd.com/news/new-biomarker-data-again-draw-eyes-mineral-oil-reduce-it); [critical review, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10184960/)
- Atrial fibrillation harm: Gencer et al., Circulation 2021 meta-analysis of 7 RCTs (81,210 participants, trials 2012–2020, >500 pts, >1 y) — omega-3 associated with ~25% increased AF risk, higher at doses >1 g/day. — [Circulation editorial/commentary](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.121.057464); [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/cardiology/20211019/omega3-fatty-acid-supplementation-associated-with-increased-af-risk)
- An updated meta-analysis of 35 trials (114,592 individuals) reports a dose-dependent AF association: <1500 mg/d EPA+DHA (possibly up to 2400 mg) appears safe regarding AF, >3000 mg/d in high-risk CVD populations warrants caution. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13489779/)

**Multivitamins**
- USPSTF 2022 final recommendation (adults, community-dwelling, non-pregnant): **D (recommend against)** beta-carotene and vitamin E for CVD/cancer prevention (beta-carotene harms outweigh benefits — moderate certainty); **I (insufficient)** for multivitamins and other single/paired nutrients. Evidence review found little or no benefit on CVD, cancer, death, except a small benefit for cancer incidence with multivitamins. — [USPSTF recommendation](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/vitamin-supplementation-to-prevent-cvd-and-cancer-preventive-medication); [JAMA evidence review](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2793447)
- COSMOS (21,442 US adults — women >=65, men >=60; median 3.6 y): multivitamin had no significant effect on invasive cancer (518 vs 535) or major CV events; a lung cancer reduction was a secondary finding. COSMOS-Mind substudy: multivitamin modestly improved cognitive test scores after 3 years (cocoa did not). — [COSMOS AJCN/PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9170475/); [COSMOS-Mind, Alzheimer's & Dementia 2023](https://alz-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/alz.12767); [COSMOS results page](https://cosmostrial.org/results/)

**Creatine**
- Systematic review/meta-analysis (Xu et al., Frontiers in Nutrition 2024): creatine monohydrate improved memory, attention time, and processing speed in adults, but effects depended on age, sex and health status; no significant effect on global cognition or executive function. — [Frontiers / PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11275561/)
- Meta-analysis of RCTs in healthy individuals (Prokopidis et al., Nutrition Reviews 2023): memory improvement was significant in older adults (66–76 y) vs younger participants. — [Nutrition Reviews](https://academic.oup.com/nutritionreviews/article/81/4/416/6671817)
- Narrative review (2025/26): creatine combined with resistance training improves strength, lean mass, functional capacity in older adults; cognitive effects modest, larger with low baseline creatine (e.g. vegetarians). Narrative, not systematic. — [PMC narrative review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12832544/)

**Magnesium**
- Meta-analysis (BMC Complement Med Ther 2021): 3 RCTs, 151 older adults with insomnia; sleep-onset latency 17.36 min shorter vs placebo; total sleep time +16 min (not significant); trials at moderate-to-high risk of bias, low-to-very-low certainty. — [Mah & Pitre, BMC](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12906-021-03297-z)
- 2026 systematic review of RCTs of stand-alone oral magnesium for sleep in adults: evidence remains mixed. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42661485/)
- Recent placebo-controlled RCT (magnesium bisglycinate, healthy adults reporting poor sleep): greater reduction in Insomnia Severity Index vs placebo, small effect size. — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12412596/)
- Safety: NIH ODS — Tolerable Upper Intake Level for **supplemental** magnesium in adults is 350 mg/day (based on diarrhea risk); people with renal impairment are at higher risk of adverse effects from excess supplemental magnesium. — [NIH ODS Magnesium fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/)

### Inferences
- For a wellness app, "take vitamin D / fish oil / multivitamin for longevity" is not supported by trial evidence in healthy adults; presenting them as longevity items would contradict USPSTF and Endocrine Society positions. Logging/tracking user-chosen supplements is defensible; recommending them is not.
- Vitamin D: an app should not prompt routine 25(OH)D testing or "optimal level" targets (Endocrine Society 2024 explicitly declined to set targets). RDA-level intake framing is the guideline-concordant default.
- Omega-3: framing should mention AF signal at higher doses and that prescription EPA trial results (REDUCE-IT) do not transfer to OTC fish oil.
- Creatine: the most defensible "optional" supplement for adults doing resistance training (muscle/function evidence moderate), with cognition evidence labelled low certainty.
- Magnesium for sleep: "may slightly shorten time to fall asleep; evidence is low certainty"; flag kidney disease and 350 mg supplemental UL.
- Interaction rules (anticoagulants with fish oil/vitamin E; magnesium/creatine with CKD) belong in a hard "check with clinician" gate rather than fine print.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a primary source documenting fish oil/vitamin E bleeding interactions with anticoagulants (e.g. NIH ODS omega-3 fact sheet); this is commonly stated but should be verified before use in copy.
- Creatine safety in CKD / effect on serum creatinine (a lab-interpretation confounder relevant to an app ingesting labs) was not sourced in this session.
- Exact VITAL participant count and omega-3 dose details were taken from summaries, not the NEJM primary paper.

## 2. Routine general health checks / annual "full blood panels" in asymptomatic adults

### Takeaway
High-certainty Cochrane evidence (15 trials, ~252,000 participants) shows general health checks do not reduce total, cancer, or (probably) cardiovascular mortality; the large Danish Inter99 trial found no effect on ischaemic heart disease, stroke, or mortality at 10 years. Choosing Wisely positions oppose routine annual screening labs (CBC/metabolic panels) in asymptomatic adults; targeted, risk-based tests (lipids, diabetes screening) are what guidelines support.

### Cited Findings
- Cochrane review (Krogsbøll et al., 2019; 15 trials, 251,891 participants): total mortality RR 1.00 (95% CI 0.97–1.03; 11 trials, 233,298 participants, 21,535 deaths; high certainty); cancer mortality RR 1.01 (0.92–1.12; high certainty); CVD mortality RR 1.05 (0.94–1.16; moderate certainty). Conclusion: systematic offers of health checks are unlikely to be beneficial and may lead to unnecessary tests and treatments. — [Cochrane Library](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD009009.pub3/full); [Cochrane plain-language summary](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD009009_general-health-checks-reducing-illness-and-mortality); [ACP Journal Club](https://doi.org/10.7326/acpj201905210-052)
- Inter99 (BMJ 2014; Danish population cohort of 61,301 aged 30–60; ~13,000 invited to screening + repeated individually tailored lifestyle counselling over 5 years): no effect on ischaemic heart disease, stroke, or mortality at population level after 10 years. — [PubMed/ACP summary](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25329220/); [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Effect-of-screening-and-lifestyle-counselling-on-of-J%C3%B8rgensen-Jacobsen/0acd15078198b790687a60c1344ced794ce84e81)
- Choosing Wisely Canada: do not order annual screening blood tests unless directly indicated by the patient's risk profile. — [Choosing Wisely Canada toolkit](https://choosingwiselycanada.org/toolkit/ordering-routine-blood-work/)
- SGIM Choosing Wisely checkup recommendations (2022): advises against routine annual exams for all, recommends customizing to risk and health status; comprehensive physical exams and routine lab tests are generally unnecessary at checkups. (Summary page only; exact wording is in the linked PDF which I did not retrieve.) — [SGIM](https://www.sgim.org/resource/choosing-wisely-checkup-recommendations/); [PDF](https://www.sgim.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/Choosing-Wisely-Checkups-Recs-20220923.final_.authors-web-version.pdf)
- European Journal of Internal Medicine review "General health check-ups: to check or not to check? A question of choosing wisely" (2022) — further discussion of the same evidence base. — [EJIM](https://www.ejinme.com/article/S0953-6205(22)00450-2/fulltext)

### Inferences
- An app that nudges users toward an annual "full panel" (CBC, CMP, ferritin, fasting insulin, etc.) as a preventive act would cut against high-certainty evidence and Choosing Wisely. Better: surface guideline-indicated, risk-triggered tests; let users *store/interpret* panels they already have, with reference ranges and "discuss abnormal values with a clinician" framing.
- Displaying many analytes with "optimal range" overlays (common in longevity apps) multiplies false-positive flags: with ~20 independent tests and population-based 95% reference intervals, an abnormal result in a healthy person is expected (statistical inference, not sourced here).

### Gaps
- No specific Choosing Wisely / guideline statements retrieved this session for **ferritin**, **fasting insulin/HOMA-IR**, or **OGTT** in asymptomatic adults. I found no source establishing routine fasting insulin as guideline-recommended; flag as unverified rather than state a position.
- Quantified harms of incidental findings from routine labs (cascade rates) were not sourced for blood tests specifically (see imaging section for WB-MRI numbers).

## 3. Whole-body MRI, coronary calcium, multi-cancer early detection (Galleri), ApoB/Lp(a)

### Takeaway
Guidelines support a few targeted tests: Lp(a) once per lifetime in adults (EAS 2022 consensus / ESC/EAS), and CAC scoring for intermediate- (and selected borderline-) risk adults 40–75 when statin decisions are uncertain (ACC/AHA 2019). Whole-body MRI for asymptomatic people is not recommended by radiology bodies (high incidental/false-positive rates). Galleri's only RCT (NHS-Galleri, reported ASCO 2026) missed its primary endpoint (stage III–IV reduction), with mortality data pending; an FDA advisory panel in Sept 2026 narrowly favoured approval while noting missing evidence.

### Cited Findings

**Whole-body MRI**
- Systematic review (Kwee et al., JMRI 2019; 12 studies, 5,373 asymptomatic subjects): pooled prevalence of critical incidental findings 13.4% and indeterminate findings 13.9%; a substantial share proved false positive and verification data are lacking; authors conclude WB-MRI should not be offered for preventive screening outside research. — [JMRI](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jmri.26736); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30932247/)
- ACR statement (2023): insufficient evidence to justify total-body screening in patients without symptoms, risk factors, or family history. (Reported via search summary; primary ACR page not fetched.) — [search-surfaced via Kwee review context](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30932247/) — treat as needing primary-source confirmation.
- Distinct high-risk use case: WB-MRI surveillance is studied for germline TP53 (Li-Fraumeni) carriers — not general population. — [PMC meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10931931/)

**Coronary artery calcium (CAC)**
- 2019 ACC/AHA primary prevention guideline: in adults at intermediate (>=7.5% to <20% 10-y ASCVD) or selected borderline (5% to <7.5%) risk, if statin decisions remain uncertain, it is reasonable to measure CAC to guide the clinician–patient discussion. CAC=0 suggests withholding statin (absent other high-risk features); CAC 1–99 favours statin if >55 y; >=100 or >=75th percentile favours statin. — [Circulation guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/cir.0000000000000678); [ACC Ten Points](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/ten-points-to-remember/2019/03/07/16/00/2019-ACC-AHA-Guideline-on-Primary-Prevention-gl-prevention)

**Lp(a) / ApoB**
- EAS 2022 consensus statement: measure Lp(a) at least once in every adult's lifetime (level is largely genetically determined and stable); also measure in youth with ischemic stroke or family history of premature ASCVD/high Lp(a); preferred unit nmol/L. — [EAS FAQ, Atherosclerosis 2023](https://www.atherosclerosis-journal.com/article/S0021-9150(23)00182-X/fulltext); [Lp(a) Forum](https://lpaforum.org/eas-updates-lpa-consensus-statement/); [ACC update](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Articles/2023/09/19/10/54/An-Update-on-Lipoprotein-a)
- ESC/EAS dyslipidaemia guidelines (2019, and their update) recognise Lp(a) as a CV risk modifier and recommend once-in-lifetime measurement. — [Roche summary of ESC/EAS](https://diagnostics.roche.com/global/en/cardialogue/article/esc-eas-guidelines-lpa-cvd.html) (industry source; consistent with EAS consensus above)

**Galleri / multi-cancer early detection**
- NHS-Galleri RCT (~142,000 adults aged 50–77 in England, 3 annual screening rounds): **primary endpoint (reduction in combined stage III–IV diagnoses) was not met**. Pre-specified secondary: stage IV diagnoses reduced 9%, 22%, 26% in rounds 1–3; 16% more stage I–II diagnoses; unexpectedly more stage III diagnoses in the test arm. **Mortality data not yet available.** — [NHS-Galleri trial update](https://www.nhs-galleri.org/trial-updates/nhs-galleri-trial-reports-fewer-diagnoses-of-the-most-advanced-cancers); [Lung Cancers Today: experts react](https://www.lungcancerstoday.com/post/multi-cancer-early-detection-test-fails-to-reduce-late-stage-cancer-diagnoses); [Patient Care](https://www.patientcareonline.com/view/nhs-galleri-data-show-fewer-stage-iv-cancers-but-primary-endpoint-missed); company framing: [GRAIL press release](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/grail-reports-full-results-from-nhs-galleri-trial-demonstrating-substantial-reduction-in-stage-iv-cancer-diagnoses-at-2026-asco-annual-meeting-302786216.html)
- Commentary: "More Questions Than Answers in Trial of GRAIL's Galleri" (2026). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41817436/)
- FDA advisory committee (Sept 2026) on Galleri PMA: unanimous that the test is safe; 6–4 on effectiveness; 7–2 (1 abstention) that benefits outweigh risks; critics noted it misses ~85% of stage I cancers and there are no mortality data. Galleri is currently sold as a non-FDA-approved laboratory-developed test; FDA decision pending. — [MDDI](https://www.mddionline.com/ivd/fda-advisory-panel-narrowly-backs-multi-cancer-blood-test-despite-effectiveness-concerns); [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/health-news/cancer/fda-panel-narrowly-backs-multi-cancer-blood-test-despite-doubts-it-works); [NCHR comment](https://www.center4research.org/nchr-fda-public-comment-galleri-cancer-screening-test/); [American Cancer Society MCD page](https://www.cancer.org/cancer/screening/multi-cancer-early-detection-tests.html)

### Inferences
- Lp(a) once-in-lifetime is the strongest "extra test" candidate for surfacing to all adults (guideline-backed in Europe; clinician-ordered). ApoB is reasonable to display when present in labs as a risk-enhancing marker, but I did not source a guideline mandating routine ApoB testing in all adults.
- CAC should be surfaced only as conditional ("if your clinician estimates intermediate risk and statin decision is uncertain"), not as a general longevity scan; it involves radiation and cost.
- WB-MRI: defensible only as "not recommended for people without symptoms; high rate of incidental/false-positive findings".
- Galleri/MCED: should be framed as "investigational for population screening; does not replace guideline screening; RCT missed primary endpoint; no mortality evidence; FDA decision pending" — and the app must revisit when FDA decides.

### Gaps
- ACR's 2023 statement and any Society of Radiologists/RSNA positions were not fetched directly.
- No guideline text retrieved for routine ApoB in all adults (some guidelines prefer ApoB in hypertriglyceridaemia/diabetes/obesity — unverified here).
- USPSTF has no MCED recommendation (not verified this session).
- Galleri false-positive rate/PPV figures from NHS-Galleri were not obtained (only described as "consistent with prior studies").

## 4. Exercise/strength, sleep, VO2max as longevity targets

### Takeaway
Physical activity including muscle strengthening is squarely guideline-backed (WHO 2020). Cardiorespiratory fitness (VO2max) is one of the strongest observational predictors of mortality, but "training to a VO2max percentile target" (Attia's framing) is an extrapolation from cohort data — improving fitness via guideline activity is evidence-concordant; specific numeric VO2max targets for longevity are not guideline-endorsed.

### Cited Findings
- WHO 2020 guidelines (adults 18–64): 150–300 min/week moderate or 75–150 min vigorous aerobic activity (or combination); muscle-strengthening at moderate+ intensity involving all major muscle groups on >=2 days/week; additional benefit beyond 300 min moderate / 150 min vigorous; older adults (65+) additionally >=3 days/week multicomponent activity emphasising balance and strength. — [WHO guidelines (BJSM/PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7719906/); [WHO guideline document](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/faa83413-d89e-4be9-bb01-b24671aef7ca/content)
- Lang et al. 2024 (BJSM) umbrella review, 199 cohort studies, >20.9 million observations: each 1-MET higher cardiorespiratory fitness associated with 11–17% lower all-cause mortality; high vs low CRF associated with 41–53% lower risk of premature mortality. Observational. — [summary via search; cohort dose-response meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35022163/); [JAMA 2009 Kodama meta-analysis](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/1108396)
- Combined CRF and muscle strength associate with lower mortality risk (cohort). — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6153509/)

### Inferences
- Defensible app framing: WHO activity minutes and 2x/week strength as core "guideline" items; VO2max estimate (from wearables) as an *informational fitness indicator* with "higher fitness is associated with longer life in observational studies" — not as a diagnostic or target with mortality promises. Wearable VO2max estimates add measurement error.
- Sleep: I did not retrieve a guideline in this session; widely cited AASM/SRS adult 7+ hours consensus should be verified by whoever owns sleep content.

### Gaps
- Lang 2024 BJSM primary URL was not fetched (numbers from search summary; the PubMed link above is a separate 2022 dose-response meta-analysis). Verify before quoting.
- No RCT evidence found that targeting VO2max percentiles (vs. meeting activity guidelines) changes mortality.
- Sleep duration guideline sources not collected.

## 5. How do Huberman / Attia / Johnson claims compare with the evidence? Systematic analyses of influencer accuracy?

### Takeaway
I found no peer-reviewed systematic accuracy audit of Huberman, Attia, or Johnson content. Available material is journalistic critique and one qualitative content analysis of the Huberman podcast (engagement, not accuracy). The pattern from critiques: core lifestyle advice (exercise, sleep, diet quality) aligns with guidelines; supplement stacks, extensive biomarker testing, WB-MRI, and off-label drugs (e.g. rapamycin) go well beyond evidence, often with commercial conflicts.

### Cited Findings
- Tsulukidze, Grande, Naslund (JMIR Formative Research, Dec 2023): qualitative content analysis of 23 Huberman Lab episodes and viewer comments; describes a "direct-to-consumer research translation" model with very high engagement; did **not** assess accuracy, and authors call for study of measurable health impacts. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10731552)
- Slate (2024, scientist-authored critique): Huberman's content is mostly helpful but mixed with complex protocols lacking scientific support and conflicts of interest; frequently extrapolates animal/in-vitro data to human protocols. — [Slate](https://slate.com/technology/2024/03/andrew-huberman-huberman-lab-health-advice-podcast-debunk.html) (opinion/journalism)
- Critique of Attia: much advice reasonable (exercise, sleep, whole foods) but paired with intensive monitoring, extensive blood testing, whole-body MRI, genetic testing and unproven drugs lacking strong evidence, with commercial interests. — [Katie Couric Media opinion](https://katiecouric.com/news/opinion/cbs-news-peter-attia-health-contributors/) (opinion)
- Bryan Johnson: no RCT has tested the Blueprint composite protocol (100+ compounds); it is an n-of-1 self-experiment. Secondary sources report he stopped rapamycin in Sept 2024 citing skin/soft-tissue infections, lipid and glucose elevations, and higher resting heart rate. — [Beyondd overview](https://www.beyondd.com/en/blog/bryan-johnson-blueprint/); [Longetivities blog](https://longetivities.com/blog/bryan-johnson-rapamycin-and-the-blueprint-protocol-mtor-biological-age-and-t-cel/) — **low-quality/commercial blog sources; verify against Johnson's own statement before citing.**
- Market influence (marketing-firm research, not peer-reviewed): brands cited by top long-form wellness hosts appear in AI-engine answers 6.8x more than matched controls. — [5WPR study](https://www.5wpr.com/research/podcast-citation-effect/wellness/) (low evidentiary weight)

### Inferences
- The app should not attribute recommendations to personalities; map each item to its evidence grade instead. Where influencer items overlap with guidelines (strength training, aerobic fitness, sleep regularity, Lp(a) once), the guideline source is the better citation.
- Items primarily originating from influencer protocols (rapamycin, metformin for longevity, NAD precursors, large supplement stacks, WB-MRI, routine full panels) lack outcome RCTs in healthy adults.

### Gaps
- No systematic, peer-reviewed accuracy study of these specific influencers found. (General literature on health misinformation on YouTube/TikTok exists but was not retrieved this session.)
- Johnson's rapamycin discontinuation not verified from a primary source.

## 6. Ethical/safety framing for "optional"/"experimental" items

### Takeaway
GRADE provides tested plain-language wording to convey certainty ("will / probably / may / the evidence is very uncertain"), and RCT evidence shows consumers can receive uncertainty statements in written health information. This supports an app pattern where every non-guideline item carries an explicit certainty label and harms, rather than being hidden or promoted.

### Cited Findings
- GRADE guidelines 26 (J Clin Epidemiol 2020): informative statements combine effect size and certainty — high/moderate/low/very low conveyed as "will", "probably", "may", "we are uncertain"; acceptable to >60% of surveyed producers/users for low–high certainty; respondents preferred "the evidence is very uncertain" over "we are uncertain". — [J Clin Epidemiol](https://www.jclinepi.com/article/S0895-4356(19)30416-0/fulltext)
- Web-based parallel-group RCT on communicating uncertainty in written consumer health information (JMIR 2020), using GRADE categories of uncertainty (risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision). — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7445603/)
- Cochrane health-check evidence explicitly names unnecessary tests and treatments as the expected harm of untargeted checking. — [Cochrane](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD009009.pub3/full)

### Inferences (proposed framing for openhealth)
- Tiering: (A) **Guideline-backed** (WHO activity incl. strength; Lp(a) once — EAS/ESC; guideline screening owned elsewhere). (B) **Conditional / clinician decision** (CAC if intermediate risk; ApoB displayed as risk marker). (C) **Low-certainty optional** (creatine with training; magnesium for sleep onset; RDA-level vitamin D) — show "may" language, effect size, harms, interaction gates. (D) **Not recommended for people without symptoms** (WB-MRI, routine full panels, routine 25(OH)D, routine high-dose fish oil/multivitamin for longevity, beta-carotene/vitamin E — USPSTF D). (E) **Investigational** (Galleri/MCED pending FDA, off-label longevity drugs) — informational only, no nudges.
- Never default-on reminders for tiers C–E; require user opt-in; show contraindication checks (anticoagulants, kidney disease, pregnancy) before any supplement plan item.
- Use GRADE phrasing and state population mismatch (most trials in adults 50–60+; extrapolation to 18–40-year-olds is indirect).

### Gaps
- No empirical study found specifically on how consumer apps labelling items "experimental/optional" affects uptake or harm; the framing above is inference from GRADE communication research.
