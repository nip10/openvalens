# Long-term personal goal aggregation

## How should progress on multiple personal goals be summarized?

### Takeaway
There is no empirical basis here for claiming either equal weighting or importance/difficulty weighting is scientifically optimal for a general-wellness product. Goal Attainment Scaling (GAS) offers a precedent for prospectively defining individualized goal levels and optionally weighting goals, but the resulting sum depends on strong measurement and implementation assumptions, with uneven cross-setting validation. A prudent product design keeps domain-level progress visible and treats any single aggregate as an explicitly chosen, descriptive summary—not a validated health score.

### Inferences
- If a single number is required for product utility, equal-weight averaging of per-goal attainment fractions is a transparent *product option*, not an evidence-established optimum. It avoids hidden value judgments about domain importance, but still implies goals are commensurable and fully compensatory.
- A user-selected priority weight is another product option, closer to GAS’s individualized-value rationale. It should be elicited explicitly and be optional; no evidence found establishes user weights as more valid/reliable than equal weights, and weighting can magnify unstable or changing preferences.
- Importance and difficulty are distinct concepts. GAS weights were described as relative value, while difficulty is principally handled by constructing expected outcome levels. Combining difficulty into weight risks counting it twice and has no support from the sources reviewed.
- To reduce compensation blindness, display each goal/domain outcome alongside the aggregate, flag meaningful worsening separately, and avoid letting improvement in one goal erase deterioration in another without an explicit user-chosen tradeoff. This is a design safeguard inferred from the mathematics, not a clinical threshold.
- Fix the baseline, goal definition, target, and evaluation window for each scoring period. If goals or baselines change, start a new version/period and annotate it rather than presenting the resulting number as an uninterrupted comparable trend. GAS literature notes longitudinal comparability problems when scales differ; it does not establish a universal re-baselining protocol.
- Do not interpret a goal-attainment composite as overall health. For the selected sleep/activity/nutrition/body/wellbeing domains, neither shared units nor clinically justified cross-domain weights are established by GAS evidence.

### Gaps
- No verified study directly compares equal-weight versus user-importance-weighted aggregation for multiple personal wellness goals in generally healthy adults.
- No evidence found defines an empirically optimal number of concurrent wellness goals, a universal goal prioritization rule, or a validated method for handling goal changes and missing observations in this product context.
- The sources do not establish robust safeguards or quantitative thresholds for ceiling effects or gaming in consumer self-tracking. GAS literature identifies easy/poorly constructed goals and leading/selective scoring as validity risks, but no general-wellness rates or formula fixes were verified.
- Cross-domain compensability is a normative/product choice, not a solved statistical fact. Whether “sleep goal up, activity goal down” should net to neutral depends on user intent; science reviewed does not provide universal exchange rates.
- Clinical GAS evidence transfers cautiously: its adult trial literature is heavily rehabilitation/geriatrics, and its original use was mental health program evaluation. It does not validate wellness targets, domain normalization, or aggregate health interpretation.

## What measurement and scoring constraints should govern any aggregate?

### Takeaway
An aggregate is only interpretable if the goal scales and their scoring process are explicit and stable. GAS illustrates useful prospective anchoring, but its canonical standardized score is not a plug-in wellness formula: it depends on constructed ordinal levels, expected values, inter-goal correlations, and implementation quality that consumer data may not satisfy.

### Cited Findings
- GAS originally specifies observable levels around a defined expected outcome and constructs a composite from per-goal scores; original authors caution that unrealistic scale construction changes the composite distribution and that scores may not be comparable longitudinally or across programs using different scale procedures. [Kiresuk & Sherman](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01530764)
- In the adult RCT scoping review, reported scoring varied (raw, T-score, other or unreported); two-thirds omitted how GAS scores were calculated, and most did not report training or calibration. Authors say this heterogeneity makes interpretation/comparison challenging and can threaten robustness. [Logan et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9316030/)
- The measurement-properties systematic review judged evidence insufficient to assess GAS validity, despite reported responsiveness, particularly in drug trials; generalizing psychometric evidence across settings is not warranted. [Gaasterland et al.](https://bmcmedresmethodol.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12874-016-0205-4)
- The original GAS scoring and the use of arithmetic on assigned category values do not by themselves demonstrate that distances between outcome levels are equal. Later methodological critique identifies this as a concern; full paper not consulted here. [Tennant](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17882728/)
- GAS in practice often relies on self-report or incompletely reported scoring: Logan et al. found 21% self-reported attainment, 45% with scoring method not reported; their discussion notes self-report can be vulnerable to imprecision and assessor ratings may also differ. [Logan et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9316030/)

### Inferences
- Before aggregating, define for every goal: measure and unit, baseline date/value, direction of improvement, target, time horizon, source (manual/device), missingness status, and scoring rule. A ratio such as “fraction of target reached” is not automatically meaningful when baseline, direction, or target differs.
- Prefer showing raw per-goal progress and the exact formula. Do not present GAS T-score, a 0–100 composite, or a normalized weighted score as validated unless the scoring assumptions and intended population have actually been tested.
- Keep missing, not applicable, paused, and zero progress distinct. Excluding a missing goal changes the denominator and can inflate the aggregate; treating it as zero penalizes incomplete data. State the rule and show coverage.
- Avoid unbounded accumulation of already-achieved targets or repeated easy goals: freeze goal versions per period, preserve achievement history, and make target edits auditable. These are practical anti-ceiling/gaming options, not evidence-backed prevalence claims.
- A single prioritized goal or a small separately reported set may be more interpretable than a composite. IOM provides a mental-health example of one personally relevant goal alongside a standardized measure, but does not settle wellness product design.

### Gaps
- No direct validation of linear percentage-to-target progress, averaging across unlike units, or any particular 0–100 transformation across the five product domains was found.
- There is no validated baseline length, minimum number of observations, recalculation cadence, ceiling correction, or missing-goal threshold for these personal wellness goals.
- Research reviewed does not establish that goal attainment is equivalent to behavior change, health improvement, or medical benefit. Keep “progress toward your selected goals” as the construct label.
