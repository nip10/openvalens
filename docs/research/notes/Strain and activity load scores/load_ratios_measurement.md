# Load ratios, injury/overtraining claims, and measurement constraints for a wellness strain/activity-load score

Scope: ACWR, load spikes, Foster monotony/strain, IOC and ECSS/ACSM consensus positions, wearable measurement accuracy during exercise, and wellness-vs-medical claim framing. Out of scope: PAI/mortality and TRIMP/session-RPE formulas (other researchers). Research date: 2026-10-02. Evidence type and population are noted for each claim.

## 1. ACWR: origin, critiques, statistical issues, consensus, and non-athlete evidence

### Takeaway
ACWR came from observational cohorts in elite team-sport athletes (Hulin/Gabbett, 2014-2016) and was picked up by the 2016 IOC consensus. Since 2019, methodology papers have argued that the ratio is mathematically coupled, that its time windows are arbitrary, and that the injury associations may be statistical artifacts. No consensus now supports ACWR as an injury-prevention tool. I found no ACWR evidence in general non-athlete adults. The nearest evidence comes from novice recreational runners, it measures week-to-week progression of running distance rather than ACWR, and its results were borderline.

### Cited Findings
- **Origin and model.** Gabbett, "The training-injury prevention paradox: should athletes be training smarter and harder?" *BJSM* 2016;50(5):273-80, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2015-095788. Narrative review/model built on elite team-sport data. It argues that athletes accustomed to high loads have fewer injuries, that non-contact injuries come from inappropriate programming rather than from training itself, and that "excessive and rapid increases in training loads" probably cause a large share of non-contact soft-tissue injuries. — [PubMed 26758673](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26758673/)
- **The "sweet spot".** Hulin et al. (2014-2016, elite cricket/rugby league/AFL cohorts) proposed a "sweet spot" of 0.8-1.3. Impellizzeri et al. report that Hulin's own data showed no U-shaped relationship. The U-shape appeared only after unpublished AFL data were added. A retraction request was refused because the figure was "illustrative," but the authors say errors were confirmed. (These are the critics' claims. I did not check them against the originals.) — [Impellizzeri et al., J Athl Train 2020 (PMC7534938)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7534938/)
- **Mathematical coupling.** Lolli et al., *BJSM* 2019, "Mathematical coupling causes spurious correlation within the conventional acute-to-chronic workload ratio calculations." Simulation study. Because the acute week is part of the chronic window, the method produces a spurious acute-chronic correlation of about 0.5 (r = 0.52 in 1,000 simulated athletes) even when there is no true physiological relationship. — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/320847733_Mathematical_coupling_causes_spurious_correlation_within_the_conventional_acute-to-chronic_workload_ratio_calculations); follow-up discussion in [IJSPP 2020 comment, PubMed 32240976](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32240976/)
- **Uncoupling does not fix the ratio.** Impellizzeri et al. say Lolli's later work showed "the ACWR failed to normalize the numerator for the denominator" even with uncoupled or adjusted versions, which adds noise and more room for artifacts. — [PMC7534938](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7534938/)
- **Simulated artifacts.** Bornn et al., as cited by Impellizzeri: 1,000 simulated seasons with no built-in ACWR-injury link still produced "significant" injury increases under common analysis methods (6.8% in soccer, 10.5% in American football). The effect disappeared once training schedules were taken into account. — [PMC7534938](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7534938/)
- **Arbitrary windows.** Carey et al. tested 336 window combinations and found different "optimal" windows for different metrics (3-21 days for one, 6-24 days for another), which the critics call overfitting. Four methods of calculating acute load gave 1,340-3,450 AU on the same data. Injury definitions varied (contact, non-contact, time-loss, medical complaint) and sometimes paired upper-body injuries with locomotor load. — [PMC7534938](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7534938/)
- **Conceptual critique.** Impellizzeri, Tenan, Kempton, Novak, Coutts, "Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio: Conceptual Issues and Fundamental Pitfalls," *IJSPP* 2020;15:907-913. It finds no evidence supporting ACWR in load-management systems or in recommendations meant to reduce injury, and says the ratio's statistical properties make it inaccurate and hard to interpret. It also notes that training load is not the same as tissue/biomechanical load. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/341936245_AcuteChronic_Workload_Ratio_Conceptual_Issues_and_Fundamental_Pitfalls)
- **Recommendation.** The same authors (J Athl Train 2020;55(9):893-901, "Training Load and Its Role in Injury Prevention, Part 2") advise practitioners to stop using ACWR quantitatively for injury prevention, to rely on progressive-overload principles and athlete response monitoring, and to report absolute risk alongside relative risk. — [PMC7534938](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7534938/)
- **Reanalysis.** Wang et al., "Analyzing Activity and Injury: Lessons Learned from the Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio," *Sports Med* 2020. Statistical/methods paper (author list Wang, Vargas, Stokes, Steele, Shrier is from memory, not verified). Secondary sources report that the ACWR-injury relationship disappeared when outliers were removed and ACWR was treated as continuous. — [PubMed 32125672](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32125672/) (summary from search snippet; full text not read)
- **Field status in 2021.** Zouhal et al., Frontiers in Physiology editorial, 2021. Describes the field as split. Myers et al. 2020 claimed "level A evidence" for sRPE-ACWR in elite athletes, while others reported that ACWR spikes were unrelated to later injury in professional soccer. The editorial covers athletes only and does not mention non-athletes. — [PMC8138569](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8138569/)
- **Inconsistent quality ratings.** Systematic reviews of ACWR and injury apply study-quality ratings inconsistently ("Variability in the Study Quality Appraisals Reported in Systematic Reviews on the ACWR and Injury Risk," *Sports Med* 2020). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-020-01333-5)
- **Nearest non-athlete evidence (novice runners, not ACWR).** Nielsen et al., *JOSPT* 2014. Prospective cohort of 874 healthy novice runners with GPS watches; 202 injuries. Runners who increased weekly distance by more than 30% versus less than 10% had HR 1.59 (95% CI 0.96-2.66; p = .07) for "distance-related" injuries only. There was no significant difference for all running injuries combined. — [JOSPT](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2014.5164)
- **Related runner evidence.** A systematic review of training-load change and running injuries ([PMC6253751](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6253751/)) and a half-marathon cohort ([JOSPT 2019](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2019.8541)) also exist. I did not read either in full, so their conclusions are not reported here.

### Inferences
- Showing an "ACWR" number with a 0.8-1.3 "safe zone" or a "danger zone," or calling it injury risk, would turn a contested elite-sport heuristic into a quantitative risk claim for a population in which it was never studied. A general-adult wellness app should not show ACWR as an injury-risk metric.
- A defensible alternative is descriptive: "this week's load compared with your recent 4-week average," shown as a trend with an absolute value, plus a plain-language note when week-over-week volume rises sharply. Call it "a bigger jump than usual," not "injury risk." If a ratio is computed at all, an uncoupled version (acute excluded from chronic) or an EWMA is less artifact-prone, but it is still not validated for injury.
- The novice-runner data support at most generic progressive-overload advice ("increase gradually"), which matches general exercise guidance. They do not support a numeric threshold.

### Gaps
- I found no ACWR or injury study in general, non-athlete, multi-activity adults (step- or HR-based load). The searches only returned athlete studies.
- I did not read the Hulin 2014/2016 and Wang 2020 primary texts directly. The details above come from critics and summaries.
- I found no formal post-2021 consensus statement (IOC or other) that explicitly withdraws ACWR. The "current consensus" described here comes from methodology critiques and editorials, not a new official position.

## 2. Foster monotony and strain

### Takeaway
Monotony (mean daily load ÷ SD of daily load over a week) and strain (weekly load × monotony) come from one small observational study of 25 experienced athletes, with banal illness as the outcome. Since then they have been used descriptively in sport, and no validation in general adults turned up.

### Cited Findings
- **Source study.** Foster C., "Monitoring training in athletes with reference to overtraining syndrome," *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 1998;30(7):1164-1168. Observational, N = 25 experienced athletes. Training was recorded by session-RPE × duration. Illnesses were correlated with load (rolling 6-week average), monotony (daily mean/SD), and strain (load × monotony). — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Monitoring-training-in-athletes-with-reference-to-Foster/59629df5a87418e5653956d54bf8630a102544ae)
- **Reported result.** A high percentage of illnesses could be accounted for when individual athletes exceeded individually identifiable thresholds, mostly of strain. Secondary summaries cite 77% of illnesses explained by monotony spikes and 89% by strain spikes. These figures come from search summaries, not the full text. — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Monitoring-training-in-athletes-with-reference-to-Foster/59629df5a87418e5653956d54bf8630a102544ae)
- **Later use.** Later work applied monotony and strain to non-contact injury in professional football (for example, "Workload monotony, strain and non-contact injury incidence in professional football players"). This is still an elite-athlete population. — [ResearchGate](https://researchgate.net/publication/327005862_Workload_monotony_strain_and_non-contact_injury_incidence_in_professional_football_players)

### Inferences
- The thresholds in Foster's study were individual and found after the fact. No universal cut-off exists to port into an app.
- Monotony is unstable when daily load is near-constant (SD close to 0 sends the ratio toward infinity) and undefined when SD = 0. This is a mathematical property of the formula. A general-adult user with steady daily steps would get very high "monotony" values that mean nothing physiologically. Any implementation needs guards (minimum SD, minimum days with data) and should be presented descriptively ("your week was very uniform") without illness or overtraining claims.
- The original outcome was "banal illness," a self-reported overtraining marker in athletes, so it cannot back claims about illness risk in the general population.

### Gaps
- I did not obtain the full text of Foster 1998, so the exact threshold method and the 77%/89% figures are unverified.
- I found no validation of monotony or strain built from wearable data (steps or HR) in non-athletes.

## 3. IOC load-injury consensus (Soligard 2016) and the overtraining syndrome consensus (Meeusen 2013)

### Takeaway
The 2016 IOC consensus (an expert statement for elite sport) endorsed load monitoring and specifically mentioned the <10% weekly increase rule and a 0.8-1.3 ACWR. Both ideas have since been disputed. The 2013 ECSS/ACSM overtraining statement says overtraining syndrome is a diagnosis of exclusion and that no single marker meets the criteria for general acceptance. That strongly argues against any app claiming to detect "overtraining."

### Cited Findings
- **Soligard et al. 2016.** "How much is too much? (Part 1) International Olympic Committee consensus statement on load in sport and risk of injury," *BJSM* 2016. Expert consensus on elite and Olympic sport. It defines load broadly (rapid changes in training/competition load, congested calendars, psychological load, travel) and gives guidance on prescribing load and on monitoring training, competition and psychological load, wellbeing, and injury. — [IOC PDF](https://stillmed.olympics.com/media/Documents/Athletes/Medical-Scientific/Consensus-Statements/2016_How-much-is-too-much-load-sport-risk-illness-part-1.pdf); [OSTRC PDF](https://www.ostrc.no/globalassets/publications/soligard_2016_bjsm_how-much-is-too-much.-ioc-consensus-statement-on-load-in-sport-and-risk-of-injury.pdf)
- **The two numeric rules.** The statement includes "weekly increments in training load should be less than 10%" and an ACWR "ideally" between 0.8 and 1.3. — [search summary of Soligard 2016](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/306271510_How_much_is_too_much_Part_1_International_Olympic_Committee_consensus_statement_on_load_in_sport_and_risk_of_injury); the 2021 editorial confirms the IOC statement "suggests the use of the ACWR approach for injury prevention" amid controversy — [PMC8138569](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8138569/)
- **Companion statement on illness.** Schwellnus et al. 2016, Part 2, covers load and illness risk. — [PDF](https://www.klokeavskade.no/globalassets/publications/schwellnus_2016_bjsm_how-much-is-too-much_ioc-consensus-statement-on-load-in-sport-and-risk-of-illness.pdf)
- **Meeusen et al. 2013.** "Prevention, diagnosis and treatment of the overtraining syndrome: Joint consensus statement of the ECSS and ACSM," *Eur J Sport Sci* 2013;13(1):1-24. Authors: Meeusen, Duclos, Foster, Fry, Gleeson, Nieman, Raglin, Rietjens, Steinacker, Urhausen. It describes a continuum: functional overreaching (recovery in days), non-functional overreaching (weeks to months), and overtraining syndrome (months or longer). Hormonal, performance, psychological, biochemical, and immune markers are all used, but "none of them meets all criteria to make its use generally accepted." — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Prevention,-diagnosis-and-treatment-of-the-Joint-of-Meeusen-Duclos/66660afacc013f8c12bf1a86b46f7ea191a69e31); [Loughborough repository](https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/Prevention_diagnosis_and_treatment_of_the_overtraining_syndrome_Joint_consensus_statement_of_the_European_College_of_Sport_Science_ECSS_and_the_American_College_of_Sports_Medicine_ACSM_/9627380)

### Inferences
- Both statements are written for athletes in supervised settings. The IOC's ACWR guidance predates the 2019-2021 critiques and cannot be cited as current support for ACWR thresholds.
- In the ECSS/ACSM framework, "overtraining" is a clinical diagnosis reached after excluding other causes, which may be organic disease. An app labeling a user as "overtrained" would effectively be implying a diagnosis. Wording like "your recent training load is higher than your usual; consider how you're recovering" stays descriptive.

### Gaps
- I did not read the full Soligard text, so I can't quote its exact wording on the evidence grade behind the 10% rule or list every monitoring tool it names.
- I found no updated IOC load-injury consensus after 2016 that revises the ACWR guidance.

## 4. Accuracy of wearables during exercise and what it means for HR-zone time and load

### Takeaway
Wrist optical HR is reasonably accurate at steady state and in low-arm-motion exercise such as cycling. Error grows with activity, rhythmic arm motion, and intensity, and it varies by device. Two studies disagree on whether accuracy also depends on skin tone, possibly only at higher intensity. Energy expenditure from wearables is not accurate for any brand. Accelerometer-only intensity classification misclassifies a large share of light and moderate activity. Taken together, HR-zone minutes and calorie-based load are approximate, device-dependent estimates. They are suitable for personal trend display, not for threshold-based risk claims.

### Cited Findings
- **Gillinov et al. 2017** (*Med Sci Sports Exerc* 49(8):1697-1703). Lab study in healthy adults, ECG reference, treadmill, cycle, and elliptical. Concordance with ECG: Polar H7 chest strap r_c = 0.99; Apple Watch 0.80; Fitbit Blaze 0.78; TomTom Spark 0.76; Garmin Forerunner 0.52. Optical devices were more accurate in exercise with less arm motion. — [PubMed search](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Gillinov+variable+accuracy+wearable+heart+rate+monitors+aerobic+exercise) (citation details and r_c values come from a web-search summary. I did not fetch the primary record.)
- **Shcherbina et al. 2017** (*J Pers Med* 7(2):3). Lab study, diverse cohort, 7 wrist devices. For HR, 6 devices had median error <5% on the cycle ergometer, with others at 5.1-8.8%. For energy expenditure, no device was within acceptable error: lowest relative errors were ~31-32% for walking and running and 52.4% for sitting. — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4426/7/2/3)
- **Bent et al. 2020** (*npj Digit Med*, "Investigating sources of inaccuracy in wearable optical heart rate sensors"). Lab study, N = 53 (ages 18-54), evenly spread across Fitzpatrick types 1-6, ECG reference. Consumer devices (Apple Watch 4, Fitbit Charge 2, Garmin Vivosmart 3, Xiaomi Miband 3) had MAE 7.2 ± 5.4 bpm at rest and 10.2 ± 7.5 bpm during activity. Error during activity averaged about 30% higher than at rest. There was no statistically significant effect of skin tone, but device and activity type mattered. Some devices locked onto rhythmic motion during walking ("signal crossover"). Limitations: the activity was only walking to ~50% of max HR, participants were young (mean 25.6), and 0-43 s time lags were found. — [PMC7010823](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7010823/)
- **Criticism of Bent 2020.** Critics noted that few participants had the darkest skin type (n = 9 in type VI). — [npj Digit Med response](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-021-00408-5)
- **Hung et al. 2025** (PLoS One). Cross-sectional, N = 25 healthy young adults, Fitbit Charge 5 vs. Polar H10 chest strap. Error at rest was about 2.8 bpm in all groups. During exercise, light skin stayed around 4 bpm. Dark skin reached 7.6 bpm at moderate intensity (40-60% HRR) and 16.5 bpm at high intensity (>60% HRR). Medium skin reached 11.8 bpm at high intensity. The skin tone × intensity interaction was significant. This partly contradicts Bent 2020, though Bent never tested high intensity. — [PLoS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0318724)
- **Exercise-intensity validation, Apple Watch S6 and Garmin Forerunner 945** (PMC9445511). N = 30 healthy adults aged 20-40, maximal CPET, 12-lead ECG. At the HRR intensity cut-points, MAPE was <1% for Apple and 1.16-1.39% for Garmin, with CCC 0.936-0.979. The authors judge both devices valid for setting training zones. This was a newer-generation, fitness-oriented, controlled-lab test. — [PMC9445511](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9445511/)
- **Fuller et al. 2020 systematic review** (*JMIR mHealth uHealth* 8(9):e18694). 158 publications, 9 brands. No brand measured energy expenditure accurately. HR was more variable: Apple and Garmin were the most accurate, and Fitbit tended to underestimate. — [JMIR](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2020/9/e18694/); [PMC7509623](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7509623/)
- **Accelerometer intensity classification.** A recent systematic review of wrist trackers reports that accelerometer-only trackers have often misclassified 25-35% of activity. In its summary, accuracy was about 70% for sedentary, 45-46% for light and moderate, and 78% for vigorous activity. Light activity was often labeled sedentary, moderate intensity was underestimated, and upper-body non-locomotor movement was misclassified. — [PMC13542415](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13542415/) (figures from the search snippet. I did not fetch the full text, so treat them as provisional.)
- **Research guidance.** Published guidelines exist for using wrist-worn consumer wearables to measure HR in biobehavioral research. — [npj Digit Med 2020, s41746-020-0297-4](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-020-0297-4) (not read in full)

### Inferences
- **HR-zone time.** At steady state, a ~5-10 bpm error can move minutes across zone boundaries, since zones are often only 10% of HRR wide, roughly 12-20 bpm. The error grows in interval training, strength training (grip and wrist flexion), arm-heavy activities, high intensity, and possibly on darker skin at high intensity. Zone minutes should therefore be labeled as estimates. Where the platform exposes it, the app should record the device or source (for example, a chest strap versus wrist PPG via Health Connect) and prefer chest-strap data.
- **Energy expenditure.** Errors of roughly 30-50% or more, and brand-dependent, mean calorie-based load should not drive load ratios.
- **Steps and accelerometer intensity.** These are reasonable for walking-dominant volume trends. They miss or misclassify cycling, strength, and upper-body work, so a load score built only from steps will undercount many workouts.
- **Manual workout logs.** I found no device-validity data on manual logs (duration × self-rated intensity overlaps with sRPE, which is out of scope here). Manual logs fill coverage gaps such as strength work and swimming, but depend on how consistently users log. Missing days look identical to rest days, so data-completeness flags are needed before computing any rolling ratio.
- **Product implication.** Show load as a within-person, same-device trend with a confidence or completeness signal. That fits the repo's rule to "prefer deterministic summaries and explicit confidence/readiness signals." Avoid cross-user or absolute thresholds.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch the Gillinov 2017 primary PubMed page. The citation and r_c values come from the search snippet, though they are consistent across sources.
- I found no validation data on wrist HR during resistance training or HIIT for the specific devices that sync through Android Health Connect.
- I found no published validation of manual workout-log accuracy against objective load.
- I did not investigate whether Health Connect exposes data source or device type in a way usable for confidence weighting.

## 5. Safe product framing: wellness vs. medical claims (FDA, EU MDR)

### Takeaway
In both the US and the EU, regulatory status depends on intended purpose and claims. Descriptive activity and HR summaries for "maintaining a healthy lifestyle" fall within wellness. Naming conditions or clinical thresholds, or framing outputs as diagnosis, screening, or prevention of a specific injury or disease, moves toward medical-device territory. "Injury risk" and "overtraining" scores are the risky framings.

### Cited Findings
- **FDA guidance revision.** FDA, *General Wellness: Policy for Low Risk Devices*, finalized January 6, 2026, superseding the September 2019 version. Wearables that estimate physiologic parameters with optical sensors (for example HRV, and now non-invasive blood pressure) may qualify as wellness products when outputs are intended solely for wellness uses. — [Covington, Jan 2026](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/fda-issues-revised-guidance-on-general-wellness-products); [Cooley, Jan 2026](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-20-fda-opens-aperture-for-wearables-in-latest-general-wellness-guidance); [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/fdas-2026-general-wellness-policy-and-what-it-means-manufacturers-wearable-devices)
- **Two wellness categories.** (1) Maintaining or encouraging a healthy lifestyle, unrelated to disease. (2) Healthy-lifestyle choices that may help reduce risk or impact of certain chronic conditions where that link is well accepted. Excluded: referencing specific diseases or conditions, diagnostic thresholds or clinical values, diagnosis/screening/disease-management purposes, claims of clinical or medical-grade accuracy, substituting for cleared devices, and treatment guidance. — [Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/fda-issues-revised-guidance-on-general-wellness-products) (law-firm summary; I did not read the FDA PDF directly)
- **Out-of-range notifications.** Under the 2026 guidance, products may tell users that "evaluation by a healthcare professional may be helpful" when outputs fall outside ranges appropriate for general wellness use. Notifications must not name diseases, call outputs "abnormal, pathological, or diagnostic," or include clinical thresholds or treatment recommendations. — [Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/fda-issues-revised-guidance-on-general-wellness-products)
- **Change control.** Counsel advise aligning claims, UX, and marketing with a strictly wellness posture, avoiding features that imply diagnosis or thresholds, and re-reviewing each new feature or algorithm in case it changes the intended use. — [Berkley Life Sciences](https://www.berkleyls.com/blog/fdas-2026-guidance-expands-pathway-low-risk-digital-health-products-caution-remains-essential); [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/fdas-2026-general-wellness-policy-and-what-it-means-manufacturers-wearable-devices)
- **EU MDR and MDCG 2019-11.** Qualification depends on intended purpose as stated in labelling, instructions, and marketing. Identical software can be a device or not depending on claims. The MDR states that software "intended for life-style and well-being purposes is not a medical device." MDCG examples of non-device software include an app that informs users of their heart rate during the day. Software is medical-device software when intended to diagnose, monitor, treat, alleviate, or prevent disease, or to identify or compensate for an injury or disability. — [OpenRegulatory on MDCG 2019-11](https://openregulatory.com/mdcg/mdcg-2019-11); [Decomplix](https://decomplix.com/medical-software-mdr/); [Emergo on MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1](https://www.emergobyul.com/news/european-revision-primary-software-guidance-mdcg-2019-11-revision-1-small-changes-meaningful)

### Inferences
- **"Injury risk."** "Your injury risk is high" (or a red/green injury-risk gauge) is a prediction and prevention claim about a specific condition, an injury. Under the MDR's medical-purpose definition, and under FDA's exclusion of condition-specific and threshold claims, it would push the feature toward device status. It is also scientifically unsupported for this population (see sections 1-3). Safer framing: "Training load this week is well above your recent average," "Consider building up gradually," "Listen to your body; if you have pain, consult a professional."
- **"Overtraining."** Overtraining syndrome is a clinical diagnosis of exclusion (Meeusen 2013), so "overtraining detected" implies a diagnosis. Use "high recent load" or "recovery may be worthwhile."
- **Thresholds.** Fixed universal thresholds such as ACWR >1.5 = "danger" resemble the "clinical thresholds" FDA now flags. Prefer personal-baseline comparisons, neutral language, and an optional, disease-free "consider talking to a professional" message.
- **Open source.** The intended-purpose analysis applies to how the project describes itself (README, UI copy), even with no seller. Keeping docs and UI copy wellness-only matters.
- This is regulatory interpretation, not legal advice. A regulatory professional should confirm it before any marketing claims are made.

### Gaps
- I did not read the primary FDA guidance PDF (January 2026) or the MDR legal text directly. The statements above rely on law-firm and regulatory-consultancy summaries.
- I found no FDA or EU guidance that names "injury risk" or "training load" scores specifically. Their application to these features is my inference.
- I did not cover UK MHRA, Health Canada, or other jurisdictions.
